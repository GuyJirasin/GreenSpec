A B S T R A C T
This research explores the impact of using high-volume processed bottom ash as a substitute for cement and sand 
in high-strength concrete production, with a particular focus on durability. The study specifically assesses the 
durability of concrete by evaluating its resistance to chloride penetration, corrosion, and permeability. Initially, 
the study investigated the use of ground bottom ash (GBA) as a low and high volume cement replacement by 
varying the GBA content to 35 %, 50 %, and 65 % by weight. In the second phase, the focus shifted to promoting 
the reutilization of bottom ash as a sand replacement. This involved incorporating specimens with 50 % GBA, 
identified as the optimal cement replacement ratio from the initial phase, and replacing 60 % of the sand with 
coarse bottom ash (CBA). The findings indicated that durability properties improved with an increase in GBA 
content up to 50 %, correlating with increased compressive strength. However, increasing GBA content to 65 % 
and combining 50 % GBA with 60 % CBA showed negligible effects on the durability of high-strength concrete, 
despite a reduction in strength. In summary, the optimal GBA amount for cement substitution in high-strength 
concrete was 50 % by weight, leading to the highest compressive strength of 84.5 MPa at 90 d, the least 
weight loss from steel corrosion, and the lowest permeability.
1. Introduction
High-strength concrete have played a crucial role in the configura­
tion and providing flexibility in the design of concrete structures, lead­
ing to a substantial reduction in both structural weight and dimensions 
[1]. Nevertheless, the manufacture of high-strength concrete encounters 
specific limitations, chiefly revolving around the requirement for a 
significant cement content and a minimal water-binder (w/b) ratio. This 
requirement can lead to potential issues, such as thermal cracking, 
arising from tensile stress due differences in temperature gradient [2–4]
and autogenous shrinkage caused by the development of capillary stress 
due to self-desiccation [5–7]. Cracked concrete is susceptible to degra­
dation as the cracks create a pathway for harmful agents to infiltrate 
deeply, leading to steel corrosion [8–10]. Several studies [11–13] indi­
cate that the formation of pores and cracks within the concrete matrix 
reduces its strength and durability. Larger crack widths significantly 
exacerbate corrosion, as evidenced by the presence of corrosion pits and 
increased surface roughness. As corrosion progresses, the diffusion 
depth of chloride ions within the crack zone also increases, underscoring 
the critical interrelationship between corrosion and ion mobility. 
Furthermore, cement production is responsible for approximately 7 % of 
carbon dioxide emissions caused by human activities [14,15]. It is 
estimated that approximately 1.1–1.3 metric tons of CO2 are emitted for 
every ton of cement produced. The primary emissions stem from 
decarbonation of limestone (0.53 t) and combustion of fossil fuel for 
sintering (0.39 t) [16,17].
As the need for sustainable building practices has grown, great 
attention has been drawn to the development of high-strength concrete 
using a low carbon binder system. One effective method is to utilize 
industrial solid wastes as supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs),

reduce the demand for cement production. Fly ash is known as one of the 
most commonly used pozzolanic materials, with extensive studies con­
ducted on its effects on the properties of concrete [18,19]. Pozzolanic 
reaction in fly ash improves compressive strength by densifying of 
cement matrix through the production of C-S-H and C-A-S-H. Pormmoon 
et al. [20] observed a substantial uptick in the use of fly ash for pro­
ducing standard and specialized concrete, resulting in a fourfold price 
hike, meanwhile the utilization of bottom ash (BA) remains compara­
tively limited [21].
Similar to fly ash, BA is a byproduct produced through the com­
bustion of coal in power stations. While sharing a chemical composition 
similar to fly ash, BA distinguishes itself with a high degree of inertness, 
attributed to its considerable particle size and pronounced porosity [22]. 
The nominal particle size is estimated to fall within the range of 
0.1–5 mm, with more than 90 % of the particles retained on sieve No. 
325 [23]. This made the reutilization rate of BA is notable low, and the 
primary utilization of BA is predominantly restricted to its use as a 
substitute for road base [24]. The residual BA is discarded in landfills 
without undergoing any processes that would add value or enable 
beneficial reuse. Chindasiriphan et al. [4] documented that the Mae Moh 
power plant in Thailand generates approximately 1.4 million tons of BA 
annually, necessitating a 4.5 million USD budget for landfill disposal. In 
order to decrease the amount of BA disposed of in landfills and improve 
reutilization, it is imperative to explore additional avenues for utilizing 
BA.
Reutilizing treated BA as cement replacement has been investigated 
and proven to significantly improve compressive strength of concrete. 
Cheriaf et al. [25] proposed that that increasing the fineness of BA 
through grinding for 6 hr resulted in a significant enhancement of up to 
27 % in its pozzolanic activity. The findings align with the work of Oruji 
et al. [24] who proposed that the increase in the degree of pulverization 
of BA results in the improvement of the compressive and flexural 
strength, as well as the strength activity. This improvement is associated 
with the enhancement of pozzolanic reactions, promoting the develop­
ment of densely packed C-S-H products within the cement matrix. 
Several studies [26,27] mentioned that thermal treatment promotes the 
reactivity of BA through the formation of new crystalline phases. This 
phenomenon occurs as a result of decreasing unburn organic carbon and 
decomposing CaCO3 into CaO. Kim [28] and Kula et al. [29] suggested 
that workability loss, caused by the interlocking effect of BA resulting in 
additional water demand, can be mitigated through sieving and 
grinding. BA processed by sieving and grinding can be used as a filler to 
produce high-strength concrete and self-compacting concrete [28]. This 
is well supported by the work of Pormmoon et al. [20] who nominated 
that the fineness has a substantial effect on the quality of BA, and sieving 
prior to grinding greatly improves amorphous content in ground bottom 
ash (GBA). In 2019, Kusbiantoro et al. [30] successfully produced 
mortar with a compressive strength of 35 MPa by using 10 % GBA as a 
cement replacement. They achieved this by grinding the GBA with sul­
furic acid impregnation to increase the fineness of BA and achieve a 
specific area of 14,180 m2/kg. Several studies have examined the per­
formance of GBA as a binder replacement for producing high-strength 
concrete. Nakararoj et al. [31] focused on creep strain, reporting that 
using GBA as a binder mitigated creep, whereas using CBA as a fine 
aggregate replacement substantially increased both instantaneous and 
creep strain. Cheah et al. [32]
investigated the properties of 
ternary-blended cement incorporating ground granulated blast furnace 
slag and GBA, finding that with a suitable proportion of by-product 
material, cement mortar achieved satisfactory compressive and flex­
ural strengths. Khongpermgoson [33] analyzed the heat development 
behavior of high-strength concrete with BA as a pozzolanic binder, 
suggesting that GBA can effectively serve as a cement alternative to 
regulate concrete temperature while maintaining mechanical strength. 
Kaur et al. [34] investigated concrete containing high volumes of ground 
bottom ash, fly ash, and nano-silica, observing improved compressive 
strength, with peak values exceeding 110 MPa. Chindasiriphan et al. [4]
demonstrated the successful production of high-strength concrete by 
employing a 50 % GBA as a binder, resulting in a maximum compressive 
strength of 84.5 MPa. Moreover, their approach effectively mitigated 
thermal cracking and autogenous shrinkage, showcasing the potential of 
using GBA as a promising binder in high-performance concrete formu­
lations, which are both eco-friendly and cost-effective. These findings 
draw attention to the fact that GBA is a promising class of material for 
producing eco-friendly high-strength concrete. However, the compre­
hensive investigation in on corrosion and chloride penetration remained 
uninvestigated.
While a few studies have investigated the potential of GBA as an 
alternative binder, a comprehensive examination of its durability—­
specifically regarding corrosion resistance and chloride pene­
tration—has been largely overlooked. This focus is particularly relevant 
in Thailand, where the research was primarily conducted. In this 
context, corrosion of steel in reinforced concrete structures is the most 
common form of deterioration, often resulting in a service life signifi­
cantly shorter than the designed lifespan. Considering the importance of 
durability and sustainability in concrete applications, further research is 
needed to understand how GBA-based concrete withstands degradation 
factors. Therefore, this study addresses the research gap by examining 
the effects of substituting sand and cement with a substantial amount of 
processed BA on the durability of high-strength concrete. The physi­
ochemical properties of high strength concrete were assessed through 
compressive strength, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, and thermog­
ravimetric/differential thermal analysis (TGA). Meanwhile, the evalu­
ation on durability properties were conducted considering chloride 
penetration resistance, corrosion resistance, and permeability by means 
of water absorption. Additionally, the environmental and economic 
advantages were also comprehensively evaluated.

2.1.3. Natural aggregates, and coarse bottom ash (CBA) for use as fine 
aggregate
The coarse aggregate utilized consisted of limestone that had been 
sifted through a No. 3/8 sieve. It has fineness modulus of 6.74. The 
specific gravity and water absorption were 2.71 and 0.51 %, respec­
tively. The fine aggregate employed was natural river sand, which had 
been sifted through a No. 4 sieve—a standard mesh size with a nominal 
opening of 4.75 mm and a typical wire diameter of 1.6 mm. The specific 
gravity, water absorption, and fineness modulus of the fine aggregate 
were recorded as 2.65, 0.91 %, and 2.96, respectively. CBA, used as a

dried BA particles retained on a No. 50 sieve. These particles are the 
remaining part from the process of preparing BA for use as a cement 
replacement. It has specific gravity, water absorption and finess 
modulus of 2.22, 10.71 % and 3.38, respectively. The aggregate 
gradation for this experiment is depicted in Fig. 1. As shown in Fig. 1, 
CBA falls within the specified gradation limits for fine aggregates as 
recommended by ASTM C33 [36], indicating its viability as a replace­

Polycarboxylate-based superplasticizer (Type F) was adopted to 
control the slump in range of 175±25 mm. A slump test was performed 
following the guidelines outlined in ASTM C143 [37].
2.2. Mix proportion
The mix proportion of high strength concrete are listed in Table 2. 
The water-to-binder (W/B) ratio was kept constant at 0.25 for all mix 
proportions. The experimental program includes two series. In Series I, a 
control specimen was developed to achieve a 28-day compressive 
strength of 70 MPa, containing 560 kg/m³ of OPC. Subsequently, GBA 
was used to replace cement at weight percentages of 35 %, 50 %, and 
65 % (denoted as 35GBA, 50GBA, and 65GBA, respectively). In Series II, 
the 50GBA specimen, which exhibited the highest compressive strength 
in Series I, was selected for further experimentation. This involved 
replacing 60 % of the natural sand with CBA, denoted as 50GBA-60S. 
The use of 60 % CBA as a fine aggregate replacement was based on 
previous results studied by Chindasiriphan et al. [4], who reported 
satisfactory concrete strength and effective mitigation of shrinkage and 
thermal cracking at this replacement rate. It is noted that SP was 
employed within the range of 5–9.5 kg/m3 to attain a control slump of 
175±25 mm. The required superplasticizer dosage increased with 
higher GBA content, indicating an additional water demand associated 
with the increased GBA. Furthermore, the inclusion of CBA necessitated 
a significant increase in superplasticizer dosage, likely due to the porous 
characteristics of CBA particles.
2.3. Experimental program
2.3.1. Compressive strength
Concrete cylinders with diameter and height of 100 and 200 mm 
were casted. The demold was performed after 24 hr, and then the 
specimens were water curing until testing. The compressive strength test 
was performed at 28 and 90 d in accordance with ASTM C39 [38], using 
a constant loading rate of 0.2 N/mm²/s.
2.3.2. Chloride penetration resistance
The chloride penetration resistance was assessed using the rapid 
chloride penetration test method, following the guidelines of ASTM 
C1202 [39]. The test was conducted at both 28 d and 90 d curing in­
tervals. The cylindrical specimens, measuring 100 mm in diameter and 
200 mm in height, were dissected to produce four disc specimens, each 
50 mm high. The top and bottom discs were not used to avoid any po­
tential issues caused by bleeding and overcompaction. The middle discs 
were then placed into a water-filled desiccator under pressure less than 
6650 Pa and maintained for 3 hours. Subsequently, the disc specimens 
were coated with epoxy and placed into test cells made of acrylic blocks. 
The positive terminal was connected to the acrylic block containing 
0.3 M of NaOH solution, while the negative terminal was connected to 
the acrylic block containing 3 % NaCl solution. The constant DC current 
of 60 V was applied, and the electrical current that pass through the 
concrete sample was monitored every 30 min for 6 hr. The apparatus 
used in the experiement is presented in Fig. 2. The amount of total 
charge passed through concrete specimen is calculated as shown in Eq.

The permeability of concrete was measured using the steady-state

2.3.5. Thermogravimetry analysis (TGA)
TGA (EXSTAR6000TG, Hitachi) was used to investigate mass loss of 
powder sample through a steady increase temperature. Cement paste 
was prepared for TG analysis. At the curing age of 28 d, the cement paste 
was crushed to produce a powder sample. Then, the powder sample was 
ground until it achieved a particle size that allowed it to pass through a 
90-μm sieve. Subsequently, it was stored in a desiccator until the anal­
ysis. The experiment was conducted in a N2 environment, employing a 
consistent heating rate of 10 ◦C per minute until it reached a tempera­
ture of 1000 ◦C. The analysis was performed with 48 hours of powder 
preparation to limit any chemical shifts due to cement hydration and 
carbonation.
2.3.6. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis
The powder sample that was originally prepared for the TG analysis 
was subsequently utilized for the XRD analysis. XRD was employed to 
analyze the phase composition of each specimen using PANalytical 
X’Pert Pro powder diffractometer using Cu-Kα radiation. A scanning 
range of 2θ = 10◦-80◦was performed at a scanning speed of 0.02◦/min 
and a tube voltage of 45 kV, respectively.

likely due to the progression of the pozzolanic reaction at later ages. The 
most significant strength improvement is observed in 50GBA, which

90 d, respectively. This represents an increase of 11 % and 14 % 
compared to that of the control specimen at the corresponding curing 
ages. The result is consistence to the results of several studies [4,42]

incorporated by GBA is mainly due to the filling effect resulting in an 
increase in specific gravity. Kaur et al. [34] further support this finding 
and report that incorporating 2 % nano-silica (NS) in specimens with 
GBA promotes early strength development by enhancing the effects of 
the fillers and activating hydration processes, resulting in rapid 
compressive strength gain during the initial stages of concrete curing. 
Furthermore, it is worth noting that the GBA utilized in this study had a 
CaO content of 28.3 %, indicating its potential contribution to hydration 
and behaving similarly to cementitious materials. Another evidence that 
supports the progressive pozzolanic reactivity and its contribution to the 
enhanced long-term compressive strengths of 35GBA and 50GBA is the 
fact that their strength from 28 d of curing exceeded that of the control 
specimens. Several studies [43,44] proposed that the increase in particle 
fineness of SCMs results in a larger surface area of particles, thereby 
promoting the pozzolanic reaction [43]. This is in good agreement with 
the finding of Pormmoon et al. [20] who suggested that the process of 
sieving prior to grinding significantly enhances the quality of GBA by 
promoting the formation of amorphous content in GBA.
In contrast, 65GBA demonstrated a decline in compressive strength 
compared to the control specimens across all curing periods. At curing 
ages of 28 d and 90 d, 65GBA exhibits compressive strengths of 
66.2 MPa and 70.2 MPa, respectively. These values represent a reduc­
tion of around 9 % and 5 % when compared to the control specimens. 
Considering that 65 % of the cement was replaced by GBA, the decrease 
in strength can be attributed to two main issues arising from an inade­
quate amount of cement. Firstly, the formation of C-S-H, the primary 
contributor to strength development, is limited due to reduced hydra­
tion. Secondly, the lower level of cement hydration also results in a 
restricted quantity of available portlandite, a crucial component for the 
pozzolanic reaction. These factors combined contribute to the overall 
decrease in strength.
Fig. 4 also illustrates the impact of using CBA as a fine aggregate 
replacement in Series II. The compressive strength of 50GBA-60S was 
observed to decrease across all curing periods when compared to the 
control specimens. Specifically, the compressive strengths of 50GBA-60S 
were recorded at 68.4 MPa at 28 d and 71 MPa at 90 d. Despite the fact 
that the highest compressive strength was achieved when GBA replaced 
50 % of the cement, the inclusion of CBA aggregate at a 60 % content led 
to a strength reduction of approximately 6 % and 4 % compared to that 
of the control specimens. Furthermore, this resulted in a 15 % and 16 % 
decrease in strength compared to 50GBA. As the gradation of CBA was 
well controlled within the recommended limits of ASTM C33 [36], this 
reduction in strength potentially suggests that CBA possesses lower 
strength and less bonding stability compared to natural sand. This 
finding is consistent with previous research, which has shown that the 
use of recycled aggregates generally leads to a reduction in concrete 
strength [45–52]. It is important to note that while both 65GBA and 
50GBA-60S exhibited lower compressive strengths compared to the 
control specimens, their compressive strengths remained ≥55 MPa, 
qualifying them as high-strength concrete according to the ACI 363 
standard [53].
3.2. Chloride penetration resistance
The chloride permeability plays a vital role in evaluating the dura­
bility of concrete, as it directly relates to its resistance against chloride 
ion intrusion and corrosion caused by chloride exposure. The presence 
and connectivity of pores within concrete samples impact the flow of 
ions and, consequently, influence the current rate. Wang et al. [54]
suggested that porous samples with capillary pores will exhibit a higher 
rate of ion passage, while samples with low porosity will demonstrate a 
lower rate of ion flow. The average coulomb charge transmitted through 
specimens at curing age of 28 d and 90 d are demonstrated in Fig. 5. The 
total charge represents the amount of Cl−ions that have penetrated the 
specimen and can be used to determine the severity level. The severity 
levels were classified into five groups, such as severe (Q>4000 cou­
lombs), moderate (Q=2000–4000 coulombs), low (Q=1000–2000 cou­
lombs), very low (Q=100–1000 coulombs) and negligible (Q<100 
coulombs) [39]. The control specimens demonstrate a total charge 
passed at 28 d and 90 d of 789 and 522 coulombs, respectively. These 
values indicate that the total charge passed during both the 28 d and 90 
d curing periods is exceptionally low. The desirable properties of the 
control specimens, such as low permeability and high resistance to 
chloride penetration, can be attributed to their compact microstructure, 
which is largely a result of the low W/B ratio [55]. Previous research 
[56] has also shown that the densification of microstructures arises from 
an increased formation of reaction products. This densification of the 
cementitious matrix, facilitated by the low W/B ratio, significantly en­
hances compressive strength. Consequently, it can be inferred that 
concrete’s resistance to chloride penetration is closely linked to its 
compressive strength, consistent with findings by Aggarwal and Siddi­
que [57].
35GBA demonstrates a significantly reduced total charge passed at 
28 d and 90 d, measuring 413 and 261 coulombs, respectively. These 
values indicate a reduction of approximately 1.8 times and 2.1 times 
compared to the control specimens. On the other hand, 50GBA exhibited 
the total charge passed at 28 d and 90 d of 270 and 154 coulombs, 
respectively. This is about 2.9 times and 3.3 times lower compared to 
that of the control specimens at the identical measuring period. The 
drastic reduction in total charge passed is largely associated with the 
enhanced pore structure achieved through the appropriate use of GBA, 
leading to a reduction in both permeability and ionic diffusivity due to 
the formation of C-S-H. In addition, the incorporation of alumina- 
enriched pozzolan offers advantages in the development of hemi­
carboaluminate, which is characterized by its high filling efficiency and 
low density. This promotes the densification of the matrix, resulting in

3.3. Corrosion resistance
The weight loss and appearance of the reinforcing steel rod, due to 
corrosion accelerated by NT Build 492 [40], are depicted in Fig. 6 and 
Fig. 7, respectively. The weight loss results for the specimens cured for 
28 d exhibited a clear pattern: the control specimens experienced the 
highest weight loss at 1.27 %, followed by 35GBA at 0.91 %, and 50GBA 
at 0.72 %. The inclusion of GBA resulted in a lower weight loss 
compared to the mix without GBA. Furthermore, as the GBA content 
increased, the weight loss percentages decreased progressively. This 
indicates the beneficial effect of GBA on corrosion resistance, likely due 
to its enhanced chloride binding capacity. In contrast, when using 
excessive amounts of bottom ash, 65GBA and 50GBA-60S exhibited 
weight losses of 0.79 % and 0.8 %, respectively. This deviation from the 
previous trend suggests that higher levels of GBA content may lead to 
increased weight loss, indicating a potential inverse relationship be­
tween weight loss and the compressive strength of the corresponding 
specimen. Interestingly, the weight loss for 65GBA does not seem to 
increase in correlation with a decrease in strength. This observation 
underscores the positive impact of GBA on corrosion mitigation, as 
previously mentioned.
The weight loss observed in specimens cured for 90 d is lower 
compared to those cured for 28 d, yet they demonstrate a similar 
pattern. The control specimen exhibited a weight loss of 0.98 %, fol­
lowed by 35GBA at 0.72 % and 50GBA at 0.62 %. Meanwhile, 65GBA 
and 50GBA-60S showed weight losses of 0.71 % and 0.75 %, 
respectively. This reduced weight loss, compared to the corresponding 
specimens cured for 28 days, is attributed to a decrease in permeability 
over time. This decrease is potentially promoted by two main mecha­
nisms: (1) the enhanced degree of hydration and (2) the progression of 
the pozzolanic reaction. The corrosion results are in line with the 
chloride penetration resistance results obtained through ASTM C1202 
[39] as discussed in Section 3.2, except for 50GBA-60S. Surprisingly, 
50GBA-60S showed a slightly higher weight loss compared to 65GBA, 
despite having higher compressive strength and a lower charge passed. 
This is possibly due to the partial use of CBA as natural sand replace­
ment. Considering specific gravity and water absorption, CBA exhibits a 
lower specific gravity and higher water absorption in comparison to 
natural sand. This implies that CBA possesses higher porosity than river 
sand, resulting in an increase in the overall porosity within the concrete. 
Another assumption is that the porosity and rough surface within CBA 
may serve as a reservoir capable of accumulating salt and distributing it 
along the position of the reinforcing steel. This, in turn, can lead to 
pitting effect causing a localized degradation of the protective passive 
film and induce corrosion of the steel reinforcement [62]. This verifies 
that the localized corrosion is trendy occurred in presence of aggressive 
agent, particularly for CI−, Br−or I−. Despite the exacerbation of steel 
corrosion caused by the use of CBA, its effect is negligible. Moreover, the 
utilization of GBA as a partial cement replacement enhances corrosion 
resistance. This improvement is mainly attributed to the densification of 
the cementitious matrix and the increase in chloride binding capacity, as 
mentioned earlier in Section 3.2. Although the presence of GBA 
potentially reduces alkalinity in the system by consuming portlandite 
through a pozzolanic reaction, it effectively mitigates weight loss of 
rebar due to corrosion. This is potentially achieved through the bene­
ficial effect of GBA on reducing permeability in the cement matrix, 
resulting from the filling effect and progressive pozzolanic reaction. 
Thus, a well-developed matrix is established, ensuring the physical 
separation of the rebar from Cl−ions.
3.4. Water permeability
The coefficient of permeability holds significant importance in 
evaluating chloride penetration resistance since concrete with low 
permeability is characterized by a compact microstructure, which limits 
the pathway for chloride diffusion. Since permeability is directly asso­
ciated with the presence of interconnected pores, the coefficient of 
permeability for each specimen is quantified to validate the results of 
compressive strength and chloride penetration resistance. Fig. 8 reveals 
the coefficient of permeability (K) of concrete specimens. For specimens 
cured for 28 d, a decreasing trend in K values was observed as the GBA 
content increased from 0 % to 50 %. The control specimens exhibited a 
K value of 1.08 × 10−13 m/s, followed by 35GBA and 50GBA, which 
exhibited K values of 9.11 × 10−14 m/s and 7.96 × 10−14 m/s, respec­
tively. Meanwhile, 65GBA exhibited a higher K value of 1.21 × 10−13 
m/s, and 50GBA-60S showed a K value of 1.14 × 10−14 m/s. This sug­
gests that the K value decreases with an increase in compressive 
strength.
At a curing age of 90 days, the control, 35GBA, and 50GBA exhibited 
K values of 1.01 × 10−13 m/s, 8.33 × 10−14 m/s, and 7.56 × 10−14 m/s, 
respectively. The primary reason for the lowest K value in 50GBA is its 
superior compressive strength. Conversely, 65GBA showed the highest K 
value at 1.11 × 10−13 m/s, while the K value of 50GBA-60S was found to 
be almost similar to that of the control specimen. The coefficient of 
permeability for each specimen decreases as the curing age increases, 
which can be attributed to continuous hydration and the progressive 
development of the pozzolanic reaction over time. These processes lead 
to an improvement in particle packing and a refinement in the pore 
structure of concrete, which is a result of the space filling by hydration 
products [63]. These findings align well with the discussions on 
compressive strength, chloride penetration resistance, and corrosion 
resistance. Also, Black [64] suggested that the incorporation of silica

The C-S-H content in the control, 35GBA, 50GBA, and 65GBA spec­

corresponding specimens was measured as 72.7 MPa, 75.3 MPa, 
80.9 MPa, and 66.2 MPa, respectively. Since C-S-H is a primary factor in 
strength enhancement, the increase in compressive strength observed at 
28 d of curing may result from the synergistic effect between early-stage 
pore filling effects and the gradual progression of the pozzolanic reac­
tion. Replacing 50 % of cement with GBA may contribute to achieving 
optimal compressive strength, with the lowest total charge passed, 
minimal mass loss due to corrosion, and reduced water permeability 
compared to the control group. This improvement suggests that pore 
size was refined, and pore connectivity reduced, by replacing 50 % of 
the binder with GBA. Given the linear relationship between water ab­
sorption and permeability, a reduction in permeability is anticipated 
alongside the increase in compressive strength [74]. Additionally, the 
incorporation of GBA at optimal levels in concrete reduces permeability, 
potentially mitigating freeze-thaw damage. Refining the pore structure 
limits space for the volumetric expansion of ice crystals, with two key 
parameters influencing freeze-thaw resistance [75]: (1) compressive 
strength, which enhances resistance to stresses from expansion, and (2) 
permeability, as reducing capillary pores limits water transport within 
the concrete. Considering these factors, it can be inferred that durability 
is directly linked to compressive strength, and the use of GBA as a SCM in 
suitable amounts can improve the durability of concrete.
Meanwhile, the portlandite content in the control, 35GBA, 50GBA, 
and 65GBA was measured as 3.2 %, 1.87 %, 1.86 %, and 1 %, respec­
tively. A decrease in portlandite content was observed with increasing 
GBA content. The inclusion of GBA resulted in approximately half the 
portlandite content observed in specimens without GBA, indicating that 
bottom ash processed by sieving prior to grinding exhibits high pozzo­
lanic reactivity. Additionally, the decrease in portlandite content 
coupled with an increase in C-S-H content, observed at a curing age of 28 
d, clearly indicates that strength enhancement is partially attributed to 
the progression of the pozzolanic reaction. Conversely, 65GBA exhibited 
a significantly low portlandite content of 1 %, indicating that its lower 
strength can be attributed to insufficient cement content. This results in 
a lower degree of hydration, leading to limited portlandite formation, 
which in turn restricts the pozzolanic reactivity.
From another perspective, the portlandite has a significant role on 
determining the initiation mechanism of steel corrosion because it 
serves as a reservoir for soluble Ca2+ and OH−ions [76]. Mundra et al. 
[77] suggested that the dissolution of portlandite in the vicinity of the 
pit formation area acts as a buffer, maintaining an adequate supply of 
[OH−] ions to counteract the aggressive conditions within the pit 
boundary. This buffering effect facilitates the repassivation of the pit. 
Despite the fact that the presence of GBA consumes portlandite through 
pozzolanic reaction, it worth noting that the weight loss due to corrosion 
is well mitigated. The limited weight loss is possibly found to be asso­
ciated with the refinement in micro porosity which otherwise reduces 
permeability of concrete by providing a physical isolation of rebar from 
hazardous agents.
Although GBA contains a higher SO₃ content than cement, using it as 
a cement replacement may reduce the risk of sulfate attack. This miti­
gation is due to two factors: substantial portlandite consumption via the 
pozzolanic reaction, which lowers available portlandite for reacting 
with sulfate ions, and C-S-H formation, which densifies the cement 
matrix and restricts harmful agent penetration. Additionally, the dilu­
tion effect of GBA as an SCM lowers C₃A levels, reducing sulfate reac­
tivity. This aligns with findings by Kasaniya et al. [78], who noted that 
grinding raw coal bottom ash improves pozzolanic reactivity, sulfate 
resistance, and ASR mitigation. Since CBA originates from the same 
source as GBA, it likely shares a similar chemical composition, albeit 
with a larger particle size, suggesting a slower reaction rate.
As indicated in Table 3, the CaCO3 content for the control, 35GBA, 
50GBA, and 65GBA was measured as 1.29 %, 2.08 %, 2.37 %, and 
2.64 %, respectively. Although GBA contains approximately 35 % less 
CaO compared to OPC, it was observed that the amount of CaCO3 in­
creases with a higher GBA content. This indicates that using GBA as a 
cement replacement may potentially induce carbonation in concrete. 
Therefore, the significant reduction in portlandite content observed in 
specimens with GBA binder systems might be attributed to the combined 
effects of the pozzolanic reaction and carbonation reaction. However, 
the weight loss due to the corrosion of rebar in the GBA binder system is 
effectively mitigated, especially for 50GBA. This suggests that the 
optimal replacement content for GBA in an OPC-based system is 50 %.

3.7. Environmental and economic impacts
High-strength concrete generally requires a high cement content and 
a low water-to-binder (w/b) ratio to achieve satisfactory compressive 
strength, which presents two primary drawbacks. First, as cement pro­
duction is a major source of CO₂ emissions, traditional high-strength 
concrete is often viewed as an environmental burden. Second, the sub­
stantial consumption of raw materials contributes to increased produc­
tion costs. Table 4 presents the equivalent CO2 emissions and costs 
associated with raw materials. Fig. 11a illustrates the effects of using 
GBA and CBA as binder and sand replacements on the total cost of 
concrete. The control specimen has the highest cost at 67.5 USD/m³ . In 
comparison, 
specimens

and 
50GBA—exhibit costs of 55.9 USD/m³ and 51.2 USD/m³ , respectively, 
with 65GBA showing the lowest cost, approximately 30 % lower than 
the control due to reduced cement content. This indicates that increasing 
the GBA content effectively reduces the cost of high-strength concrete. 
In the case of 50GBA-60S, a cost reduction of around 5 % is observed 
compared to 50GBA. Although CBA is a by-product of GBA production 
and thus incurs no direct cost which is much cheaper than natural sand, 
the additional SP demand limits the overall cost reduction.
Fig. 11b presents the total CO₂ emissions associated with the con­
crete manufacturing process. The control specimen exhibited the highest 
emissions in the group, with a total of 0.61 kgCO₂/m³ , primarily due to 
its high cement content. For specimens with GBA binder, 35GBA showed 
a CO₂ emission of 0.42 kgCO₂/m³ , representing a reduction of approx­
imately 32 % compared to the control. This was followed by emissions of 
0.335 kgCO₂/m³ for 50GBA-60S and 0.334 kgCO₂/m³ for 50GBA. 
Although CBA is associated with lower CO₂ emissions compared to 
natural sand, the 50GBA-60S mixture requires an additional SP dosage, 
which has an embodied CO₂ of 1.121 kgCO₂/m³ , thereby limiting the 
overall reduction in CO₂ emissions. The lowest CO₂ emissions, at 0.251 
kgCO₂/m³ , were observed in 65GBA. The trend in total CO₂ emissions 
mirrored that of the total cost across the series, highlighting the sub­
stantial impact of cement content on both CO₂ emissions and cost. 
Although the cost and CO₂ emission benefits of CBA are limited, several 
studies highlight its advantages as a natural sand replacement. CBA has 
been demonstrated to effectively reduce heat generation and autoge­
nous shrinkage, thereby mitigating the propagation of both thermal and 
shrinkage cracks—two prevalent issues associated with concrete that 
has a low water-to-binder ratio [4,31]. Additionally, CBA’s capacity to 
mitigate crack propagation enhances long-term durability by mini­
mizing pathways for chloride diffusion, which could otherwise 
compromise the service life of concrete [8,10]. Extending the service life 
further contributes to lower life-cycle costs, offering economic benefits 
on a broader scale. Furthermore, an estimated annual budget of 4.5 
million USD is required for BA disposal. By promoting the reuse of BA as 
both GBA and CBA, the amount of BA sent to landfills can be reduced, 
providing additional economic and environmental benefits.
In summary, the incorporation of high-volume bottom ash (BA) as a 
replacement for cement and sand in high-strength concrete—typically 
characterized by high cement content and vulnerability to thermal and 
autogenous shrinkage cracks—offers significant cost reductions, de­
creases CO₂ emissions, promotes sustainable resource management, and 
enhances the durability and service life of concrete. Furthermore, given 
BA’s ability to reduce the heat of hydration, it presents a viable solution 
for producing mass concrete, where thermal cracking is a major concern. 
Additionally, BA’s potential to mitigate corrosion highlights its suit­
ability for use in concrete for marine structures. Collectively, these ad­
vantages emphasize the practical benefits of BA utilization in real-world 
construction applications.

4.1. Conclusions
This research explores the effects of using high-volume processed 
bottom ash as a substitute for sand and cement in the production of high- 
strength concrete, with a particular focus on its durability. The physi­
cochemical properties of high-strength concrete were assessed through 
tests including compressive strength, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, 
and thermogravimetric/differential thermal analysis. Meanwhile, the 
evaluation of concrete durability involved assessing resistance to chlo­
ride penetration, corrosion, and permeability. The environmental and 
economic advantages were also comprehensively evaluated. Based on 
the outcomes of these experiments, the following conclusions can be 
drawn:

1. The incorporation of GBA as a partial binder replacement in concrete 
effectively enhances compressive strength, particularly at replace­
ment levels of 35–50 %. Notably, a 14 % increase in compressive 
strength was observed at 90-d for the 50GBA specimen, achieving a 
compressive strength of 84.5 MPa. This improvement is attributed to 
the ongoing pozzolanic reactions that continue beyond the 28- 
d mark.
2. Incorporating GBA as a partial binder replacement improves chloride 
resistance by reducing water permeability and increasing chloride 
binding capacity. This is due to matrix densification from C-S-H 
formation and the contribution of alumina-rich pozzolans promoting 
hemicarboaluminate and Friedel’s salt development. The addition of 
CBA further reduces the total charge passed, highlighting that 
enhancing chloride resistance relies on increasing GBA and CBA 
content rather than solely reducing permeability.
3. The minimum weight loss due to corrosion, observed at 0.62 %, 
occurs in the specimen containing 50 % GBA. This can be attributed 
to the densification of the cement matrix when GBA is utilized. 
Additionally, it is noted that corrosion resistance is significantly 
influenced by both compressive strength and permeability.
4. GBA enhances concrete durability by reducing permeability, which 
is crucial for chloride resistance. As GBA content increases, perme­
ability decreases, with the 50GBA specimen achieving a minimum 
coefficient of 7.56 × 10−14 m/s after 90-d of curing. This reduction 
suggests that sieving BA before grinding improves particle packing 
and pozzolanic activity, transforming bottom ash into a valuable 
pozzolan. These findings underscore the potential of GBA to enhance 
concrete properties and promote sustainable construction practices.
5. TG analysis shows that portlandite content decreases with increasing 
GBA, with 50 % GBA as the optimal substitution. This correlates with 
a strength gain, reaching a maximum 28-d compressive strength of 
80.9 MPa and 7.82 % C-S-H content. Despite portlandite consump­
tion, GBA incorporation has minimal impact on corrosion, likely due 
to cement matrix densification, which isolates reinforcing bars from 
aggressive environmental agents.
6. The results indicate a notable reduction in alite peaks in the GBA- 
containing specimens, suggesting near-complete cement hydration 
by 28 d and highlighting the role of GBA in enhancing cement hy­
dration. Overall, processed bottom ash improves concrete perfor­
mance by optimizing hydration and promoting the formation of 
beneficial hydration products, leading to increased compressive 
strength and durability.
7. Incorporating GBA reduces both costs and CO₂ emissions in high- 
strength concrete. Using 50 % GBA lowers costs by 30 % and CO₂ 
emissions by 45 % due to reduced cement content, while improving 
strength. Although CBA offers limited benefits due to its super­
plasticizer requirement, it enhances durability and longevity. Uti­
lizing bottom ash as GBA and CBA reduces landfill waste and offers 
significant environmental and economic benefits.
4.2. Recommendations for future research
Future research should explore additional durability factors, 
including freeze-thaw resistance, sulfate attack, and alkali-silica reac­
tion, to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of concrete 
durability. Furthermore, long-term testing and monitoring are recom­
mended, particularly for applications exposed to harsh environmental 
conditions, to fully assess the concrete’s durability.
