A B S T R A C T   
Effect of plastic waste (PW) and graphene nanoplatelets (GNP) on properties of high-volume fly ash (HVFA) 
concrete was investigated. Response surface methodology has been used for designing experiment, modelling, 
and optimization. The variables were PW, HVFA, and GNP, and the responses were strengths and water ab­
sorption. PW and HVFA reduce the strengths and absorption while GNP enhances them. The proposed models 
developed were significant with high level of correlation. Optimized mix was achieved by substituting 15.3% of 
coarse aggregate using PW, 6.07% of cement using HVFA, and adding GNP at 0.22%, and was experimentally 
validated with error less than 5%.   
1. Introduction 
Plastic waste generation keeps increasing yearly due to increased 
population and demand of its use. This gives many countries including 
the developed nations the challenge on how to dispose the plastic waste 
effectively. In 2016 alone, it was estimated that more than 330 million 
tons of plastic waste were generated globally, in which only about 
31.1% were recycled for producing other types of plastics, 41.6% were 
utilized for energy recovery, and 27.3% were discarded as landfill. 
Furthermore, it was forecasted that by 2035 the amount of plastic waste 
generation globally will double, and by 2050 it will quadruple [1,2]. 
Discarding plastic waste as landfill have significantly negative effect as it 
is chemically unreactive to the environment and nonbiodegradable. 
Plastic waste in the landfill will not decompose and will therefore 
remain for several decades or centuries in the environment and thereby 
releasing toxic gases and elements which can pollute the air, water or 
even soil. Another option is that the plastic waste can be used as a source 
of power through burning, this can help in eliminating it, however this 
pollutes the environment by releasing CO2, toxic fly and bottom ash, and 
other poisonous chemicals. 
Some best ways of managing plastic waste are through recycling it 
into the construction industries. Applications of plastic in the con­
struction industry include using it to partially replace fine and coarse 
aggregate in concrete and in pavement to improve durability, strength, 
and serviceability [3]. Plastic waste when used in concrete will reduce 
the dependency on natural aggregate and improve environmental sus­
tainability. This is because for a typical concrete, the amount of aggre­
gate can be up to 80% of the concrete constituent materials. In addition, 
concrete demand is increasing annually due to rapid growth and ur­
banization, and the demand of natural aggregate also increases signifi­
cantly. This leads to many countries, especially the developed ones, 
facing shortage of natural aggregate, therefore utilizing plastic waste as 
aggregate replacement will overcome these challenges [4]. 
Several researchers have utilized plastic waste in concrete as coarse 
or fine aggregate replacement. Belmokaddem, et al. [5] uses poly­
propylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and high-density poly­
ethylene (HDPE) as partial substitution to fine aggregate and medium 
sized coarse aggregates in concrete. Their findings showed that for all 
the types of plastic wastes, the fresh density, hardened density, and 
compressive strength decreased with increase in content of the plastic

found to be more pronounced in HDPE concrete where there is reduction 
of up to 88% and 75% for strength and UPV, respectively. In contrast, all 
the three types of plastic aggregates improved the ductility and thermal 
insulation of the concrete. HDPE concrete showed the best performance 
in terms of ductility, and PP concrete provided the best thermal insu­
lation. Mohammadinia, et al. [6] replaced coarse aggregate with recy­
cled crushed glass (RCG) and plastic waste in concrete. They used 
percentage replacements of 0%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50% by 
volume of coarse aggregates. They also reported the decrease in 
compressive strength and tensile strengths with increment in both 
plastic waste and RCG content. Hama [7] examined the effect of plastic 
waste from drinking water bottles as partial replacement to natural 
aggregate in a two-way reinforced concrete slab. He used the plastic 
waste at 0%, 15%, 30%, 45%, 60%, and 75% by volume replacement to 
coarse aggregate. He found that all strengths decreased with increase in 
plastic waste content, except for the mixes containing 15% and 30% 
plastic wastes. Finally, he recommended up to 30% plastic waste from 
drinking water bottles can be used in concrete for structural applica­
tions. Some reasons for the reduction in strength and durability are due 
to low strength and stiffness of the concrete compared to natural ag­
gregates. Another reason was attributed to the poor bond between the 
plastic waste surface and cement matrix which leads to premature fail­
ure with little applied stress. Furthermore, plastic waste entraps air on 
its surface when mixing which restrain cement hydration reaction and 
increase the porosity in the hardened cement matrix [3,8,9]. 
Even though using plastic waste has negative impact on the strengths 
and durability of concrete, however it has some advantages as reported 
by several researchers. Plastic waste helps in mitigating heat induced 
spalling of concrete through polymer melting by releasing internal 
pressure [10,11]. It significantly improves the ductility, impact resis­
tance, and toughness of concrete. This makes it suitable for use as con­
crete barriers in highway to reduce impact of accident [12]. Plastic 
waste in form of fibers improved the bending capacity and flexural 
toughness of reinforced concrete beams, the effect was more significant 
in the tension zone. In addition, plastic waste fibers are effective in 
bridging and controlling the growth of cracks before ultimate load is 
reached [13,14]. It also reduces the thermal conductivity of cementi­
tious material due to its lower thermal conductivity compared to natural 
aggregates. Therefore, this makes it a good insulating material for 
buildings, thereby decreasing the amount of energy required for cooling 
[9,15]. 
Attempts were made to lessen the detrimental effects of plastic waste 
on the strengths and durability of concrete. In some studies, pozzolanic 
materials were used in combination with the plastic waste to mitigate 
the negative effects. Punitha, et al. [16] used plastic waste as a partial 
substitution of fine aggregate at 5%–30% by volume and replaced 10% 
cement by weight using metakaolin. They found that 10% metakaolin 
mitigated the loss in concrete strengths when up to 15% plastic waste 
has been added. Balasubramanian, et al. [17] used electronic plastic 
waste (EPW) as a partial substitute to coarse aggregate at 5%–20% by 
volume. They also replaced cement with waste glass powder (WGP) at 
5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%. They found that concrete with up to 15% EPW 
and WGP shows higher strength than the control concrete. Ali, et al. [18] 
replaced cement and fine aggregate with silica fume and plastic waste, 
respectively, each at 10%, 15%, and 20% by volume. They found that 
silica fume significantly reduced the negative effect of plastic waste both 
the tensile and compressive strengths of the concrete. Based on the re­
sults, they recommended up to 20% plastic waste to be used with silica 
fume as supplementary cementitious material. Nano silica was also 
utilized to mitigate the undesirable effect of crumb rubber in concrete 
[19,20]. By capitalizing on the advantages of plastic waste in concrete, 
and the challenges of producing concrete for structural application using 
it, therefore this study investigates utilizing another novel nanomaterial 
of the carbon/graphene family as additive concrete to lessen the loss in 
strengths and durability of concrete due to plastic waste utilization. 
Nanomaterials continue gaining acceptance in the construction and 
building industries due to their enormous advantages when used in 
cementitious composites. They serve as a nanofiller in the composites, 
thereby preventing the propagation of cracks in nanoscale. Nano­
materials also refined the cementitious matrix by filling the pores up to 
nano sizes and densified the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the 
aggregates and matrix [19–22]. Graphene nanoplatelets (GNP) is a 
nanomaterial belonging to the graphene family which is an extensive 
carbon nanomaterial. To date, research on the use of graphene nano­
materials especially GNP is still at the infant stage. Some of the char­
acteristics and properties of GNP include tensile strength of 100 – 400 
GPa, high modulus of elasticity up to 1 TPa, electrical conductivity of 
6000 S/cm, and larger surface area. Furthermore, some other unique 
properties of GNP include everlasting electrical conductivity and bipolar 
super-current. The use of GNP in cement composites improved me­
chanical properties and durability performance. These improvements 
were achieved due to pore filling ability of GNP to nanoscale, nucleation 
site effect, and formation of intercalation compounds with cement hy­
dration products. These features gives the advantage of using GNP as 
additive to cementitious materials [23]. 
Previous study by Wang, et al. [24] disclosed that addition of 0.05% 
GNP by weight of cement improved the flexural and compressive 
strengths by up to 24% and 8%, respectively. Furthermore, GNP was 
able to delay crack propagation in the cement matrix when load is 
applied. Similarly Wang and Shuang [21] used dispersant and ultra­
sonicator to disperse the GNP in water before addition to the cementi­
tious mixture. Their findings showed that the compressive strength and 
flexural strength of the concrete increased by 4.3% and 27.4% at 28 
days, respectively. Iqbal, et al. [25] studied the effect of addition of GNP 
on high strength concrete (HSC) properties subjected to elevated tem­
perature. They added GNP at 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% by weight of 
cement. Their findings revealed that the loss in residual strength due to 
elevated temperature was less in HSC containing GNP compared to those 
without GNP due to thermal efficient behavior of the GNP. Guo, et al. 
[23] treated GNP with silane and used it as additive by weight of 
cementitious materials in ultra-high-performance concrete. They re­
ported decrease in workability with increase in GNP content, while the 
compressive and flexural strengths increased with addition of GNP up to 
0.05%, where their values reached up to 142.5 MPa and 23.8 MPa 
respectively after 28 days. 
As reported, GNP significantly improves the mechanical and dura­
bility performance of concrete [23,26]. Though, the major disadvantage 
of using graphene materials in concrete is its cost, as most graphene 
nanomaterials are expensive. The smaller the sizes and reactivity of 
graphene materials such as GNP and graphene oxides, the more 
expensive it will be [27]. One of the alternatives to use GNP in concrete 
is by using it together with a supplementary cementitious material 
(SCM) like fly ash. This will reduce the overall cost of the concrete, as 
cement is the most expensive conventional material. Furthermore, 
application of fly ash to concrete has been proven to reduce the CO2 
emission into our environment which is mainly caused by the cement 
industries. In addition, fly ash has been found to increase the long-term 
ultimate strengths and durability of concrete and reduces adiabatic 
temperature rise during mixing. Conversely, the major setback of using 
fly ash in concrete is its slow pozzolanic reactivity which cause lower 
early strengths development [28,29]. Several researchers have attemp­
ted different methods to ignite the pozzolanic reactivity of fly ash at 
early ages so that the strengths of the concrete containing fly ash will 
improve even at the earlier ages. For example, few studies used nano 
silica as additive to high-volume fly ash (HVFA) concrete, and they 
found that nano silica significantly improves the early strength devel­
opment of the concrete [20,30]. Rashad [31] and Anjos, et al. [32] 
added metakaolin to HVFA concrete, they reported improvement in 
early strengths before and after heating the concrete. Wang, et al. [33] 
uses silica fume as a SCM in HVFA concrete, and they also reported 
improvement in the early and ultimate strengths of the HVFA concrete,

Based on available literature, the studies on using graphene nano­
materials like GNP to lessen the negative effect of plastic waste on 
concrete and also using GNP to enhance the early strength and devel­
opment of HVFA concrete are scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to 
investigate the effect of GNP on the mechanical properties of HVFA 
concrete. Coarse aggregate was replaced with plastic waste to improve 
environmental sustainability and conserve natural resources. In addi­
tion, HVFA was used as SCM to reduce the cost, improve ultimate 
strength, and promote the environmental sustainability [20]. GNP was 
used as additive to lessen the negative effect of plastic waste and to 
enhance strength development of HVFA concrete at early age. Response 
surface methodology (RSM) was utilized to design the experiment 
mixes, carry out the result analysis, and develop models to predict the 
properties of HVFA concrete containing GNP and plastic waste. Finally, 
mixture optimization has been carried out to maximize the mechanical 
properties of the concrete and to obtain the corresponding optimum 
HVFA, GNP, and plastic waste contents required to achieve the optimal 
properties. 
2. Response surface methodology (RSM) 
RSM has been one of the most useful tools to explain and establish 
the mathematical relation between input variables and the output re­
sponses [34]. One of the advantages of RSM is that it is more beneficial 
in the conditions where there is need to investigate the effect of several 
variables on one or more responses to minimize the number of experi­
ments required. It can also be employed where there is need to optimize 
some of the responses by setting out series of criteria for each of the 
independent variable. Furthermore, another major importance of RSM 
analysis is sufficient interpretation of experiments for the non-linear 
surface of the results experiments [35]. 
The RSM models can be in form of linear mathematical relationship 
with the variables and responses still unknown. Though, in most cases 
the data usually contains curvature, as a result the linear models be­
tween the input variables and responses are not suitable. In this case, a 
polynomial model type with higher degrees shown by the second order 
function in Eq. (1) is the most suitable [20,34].

Different model types exist in the RSM analysis package which can be 
used to generate the mathematical equations between the input vari­
ables and their responses. These model types comprise of Box-Behnken, 
Central Composite (CCD), historical, one factor, optimal, user defined 
and miscellaneous design models. Though, selection of each model type 
is reliant on the number of independent variables and its variation levels 
(number). However, the most suitable and commonly used is the CCD 
method. This is because it can predict model responses with a smaller 
number of experimental results and capturing all the variables within 
the specified ranges. In addition, in CCD model, there is a choice of 
selecting α (distance between the design center and axial run), the value 
of α depends on the total points in the factorial design section [19]. In 
CCD, each variable is varied in five levels, i.e. + α, -α, axial/factorial 
points (-1, and + 1) and mid points (central-level) [34,35]. 
For the purpose of this research, Design Expert 10 software was 
employed for the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) including 
experimental designs, mixtures proportioning, developing mathematical 
models, and optimization of the mix. The independent variables used are 
fly ash as SCM, plastic waste as partial substitute to coarse aggregates, 
and GNP as additive by weight of binder materials. 
3. Materials and experimental methods 
3.1. Materials 
3.1.1. Cement and fly ash 
Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC) Type I, which conforms with ASTM 
C150M-15 [36] requirements, has been used as the binder material. 
Table 1 presents the properties of the OPC. High-calcium fly ash (Class 
C) conforming to ASTM C618 [37] specifications, with a specific gravity 
of 2.54 and spherical in shape as presented in Fig. 1(a), has been used as 
a partial substitute to cement. The chemical properties of the fly ash 
obtained using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) are presented in Table 1. 
3.1.2. Graphene nanoplatelets 
Graphene nanoplatelets (GNP) used in this study was in grey/black 
powdered form, obtained from ACS Materials LLC, Canada. The chem­
ical and physical properties of the GNP are presented in Table 2. From 
the morphology of the GNP in Fig. 1(b), it has a sheet-like nature. 
3.1.3. Natural aggregates 
For fine aggregate, well-graded natural river sand, which conforms 
to ASTM C33 [38] specifications, in saturated surface dried form has 
been used. The fine aggregate has a specific gravity value of 2.64, water 
absorption of 0.62%, and fineness modulus of 2.8. Its particle size dis­
tribution is shown in Fig. 2. A 19-mm sized well graded gravel having a 
particle size distribution as presented in Fig. 2 has been used as coarse 
aggregate. The coarse aggregate has a specific gravity of 2.67 and water 
absorption of 0.42%. 
3.1.4. Plastic waste 
Two sizes of polypropylene plastic waste obtained from Grand Siam 
Composites Company, Thailand, were used. They are 12.5 mm (1/2 in.) 
nominal maximum size aggregate (NMSA) which is black in color and 
9.5 mm (3/8 in.) NMSA which is white in color. The particle size dis­
tribution of the plastic waste obtained by using 50% of 12.5-mm sized 
and 50% of 9.5-mm sized plastic wastes is shown in Fig. 2. 
3.1.5. Superplasticizer 
A superplasticizer of polycarboxylate based was used as a high-range 
water reducing admixture. It is in form of light brownish liquid, free 
from chloride with a pH of 4.97, and density of 1.060 g/cm3. The 
superplasticizer was used together with mixing water to effectively 
disperse the GNP to avoid agglomeration. The dosage used was 1% by 
weight of binder materials in order to adjust the effect of HVFA and GNP 
on the water-to-cementitious materials of the concrete [39].

3.2. Mix design based on RSM 
The control concrete mix (0% plastic waste, 0% fly ash, and 0% GNP) 
was designed in accordance with the guidance given in ACI 211.1–91 
[40]. The mix was designed for a targeted characteristic strength of 30 
MPa (C30/37), i.e., cube strength of 37 MPa at 28 days, using a 19-mm 
size aggregate with a water/cement ratio of 0.45. The quantities of 
materials for the control mix are presented in Table 3. 
For remaining mixes, the proportioning was obtained using Response 
Surface Methodology (RSM). The independent variables used are fly ash 
as SCM, plastic waste as partial substitute to coarse aggregates, and GNP 
as additive by weight of binder materials. The RSM software generated a 
total of nineteen (19) mixes using combinations of the independent 
variables as shown in Table 3. Fly ash (HVFA) was used to replace 
cement at 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% by volume. Plastic waste (PW) 
has been used as partial substitute at 0%, 15%, 30%, 45%, and 60% by 
volume of coarse aggregate. GNP was added at 0%, 0.075%, 0.15%, 
0.225%, and 0.3% by weight of binder materials. Each of the mixes were 
assigned a nomenclature based on the quantities of PW, HVFA, and GNP. 
For example, mix P80F40G0.15 is a mix containing 80% PW, 40% 
HVFA, and 0.15% GNP. Mix P15F20G0.225 is a mix containing 15% PW, 
20% HVFA, and 0.225% GNP. The 19 mixes consisted of 14 axial points 
and 5 center points, in which the central points are repetition of the 
central mix combination, i.e., the mix P30F40G0.15 consisting of 30% 
PW, 40% HVFA, and 0.15% GNP. These repetitive central points were 
used in the calculation of lack of fit in the RSM models. The lack of fit test 
is important in assessing the significance of the developed models, 
where it is compared with “pure error” obtained from the center point 
repetitive mixes. A good model should have a non-significant lack of fit 
[34,41]. These mixes were then prepared in the laboratory for carrying 
out experimental work. After the experimental results were obtained, 
ANOVA has been used to determine the interactions between the inde­
pendent variables (PW, HVFA, and GNP) and the measured responses. 
3.3. Sample preparations 
The constituent materials for each mix were firstly weighed and 
batched. To avoid agglomeration and obtain a dispersed GNP in the mix, 
the following steps were followed. Part of the mixing water (about 500 
g) and superplasticizer were mixed in a beaker. The GNP was then added 
to the mixture of water and superplasticizer and then stirred manually 
using a stirring rod to achieve total dissolution of the GNP particles. 
After stirring, a 60 Hz/280 W Ultrasonicator was used to further sonicate 
the suspension for 1 h, after which a uniform and homogenous GNP 
suspension was achieved. 
Mixing and curing of the concrete specimens were carried out in 
accordance with ASTM C192M-15 [42] specifications. The fine aggre­
gate, cement, and fly ash were first measured and poured into the 
rotating pan mixer and allowed to mix for up to 1 min, after that the 
coarse aggregate and plastic waste were measured and poured into the 
mixer, the dry constituents were further allowed to mix for 1 min. After 
which the remaining mixing water and the GNP suspension containing 
water, GNP, and superplasticizer, were added and allowed to mix until 
homogenous mixture was formed. The mixing process takes about 3 
mins to complete. 
Immediately after the homogenous concrete mix was achieved, the 
fresh properties of the concrete were determined. The workability was 
determined using slump test in accordance to ASTM C143M-20 [43] 
specifications, and the fresh unit weight was measured according to 
ASTM C138M [44] guidelines. Afterwards, the fresh concrete was casted 
into the molds. The samples were then kept at a temperature of 23 ± 2 ◦C 
and controlled relative humidity before demolding after 24 h. After 
demolding, the samples were immediately placed into a curing tank 
containing clean water for curing prior to age of testing.

3.4. Tests on hardened properties 
The test for compressive strength has been carried out with reference 
to BS 12390–3:2009 standard [45], using a 3000-kN Universal Testing 
Machine (UTM). Cube samples of 100 mm cubes were cast and tested 
after 3-day, 7-day, and 28-day curing periods. For each mix, three 
specimens were prepared, cured, and tested. The mean values of the 
results were then calculated and reported. 
Splitting tensile strength test has been carried out with reference to 
BS EN 12390–6:2009 specifications [46], using a 3000-kN capacity 
UTM. Cylindrical samples of 100 mm diameter by 200 mm height were 
prepared and tested for the splitting tensile strength after 3-day, 7-day, 
and 28-day curing periods. Three samples were prepared for each mix 
and curing period, and mean values were reported. 
The flexural strength test has been carried out in accordance with 
specifications to ASTM C293M-10 [47] using samples of 100 mm × 100 
mm × 400 mm sizes. Six samples were prepared for each mix and tested 
after 7- and 28-day curing period. The water absorption test has been 
carried out with reference to ASTM C642-13 specifications using 100 
mm cube samples after 28-day curing. For each mix, three samples were 
prepared and tested, and the average value then recorded. 
Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM) analysis has 
been carried out on the selected mixes to study the microstructural 
morphology of the concrete. The FESEM test was carried out using an 
ultra-high resolution SUPRA 66VP machine which analyzed and 
captured the surface morphology of the specimens down to nano size. A 
very small fracture was extracted during the 28-day compressive 
strength testing and dried. The sample was then cleaned and coated with 
gold before fixing on the copper studs with the aid of carbon tape. The 
assembly was then placed into the FESEM machine, and the images 
captured for up to 5000 revolutions. 
4. Results and discussion 
4.1. Fresh properties 
4.1.1. Workability 
The workability is one of the basic and most important factors for 
controlling the comfort and easiness when the fresh concrete is mixed, 
placed, and compacted. The results of slump test which was used to 
measure the workability are presented in Table 4. Mixes with higher fly 
ash content have high slump values, i.e., the workability improves with 
increment in fly ash addition as SCM. Therefore, fly ash increases the 
consistency and fluidity and reduces the required amount of water to 
achieve a good mix. This is due to the morphology of the fly ash, i.e., 
spherical and ball bearing shape in comparison to cement it replaced, 
which gives it the ability to reduce friction within the mixture and 
effectively disperse the paste, leading to low demand of mixing water 
and consequently higher slump [48]. 
Furthermore, mixes with high plastic waste and fly ash contents also 
show higher slump compared to those mixes with small amount or 
without fly ash. Therefore, the workability of the concrete also increases 
significantly with increment in plastic waste as substitute to mineral 
aggregates. This is mainly due to the smooth surface and low water 
absorption of the plastic waste, thereby reducing friction and amount of 
water needed to achieve consistency, and consequently improving 
slump [49]. 
On the contrary, mixtures containing higher GNP contents have 
lower slump values compared to those with lower GNP. Therefore, GNP 
addition decreases the workability of concrete. This is mainly attributed 
to its large surface area making it to absorb more water during mixing, 
thereby reducing the fluidity of the concrete [24]. From Table 4, mixes 
containing higher plastic waste and fly ash content with low or no GNP 
content have the highest values of workability and therefore they 
require less mixing water to achieve consistency.

4.1.2. Fresh unit weight 
The result of fresh unit weight of the concrete is presented in Table 4. 
Mixes containing higher fly ash and plastic waste contents have lower 
unit weight values. The fresh unit weight decreases with increment in 
plastic waste as substitute to coarse aggregate and fly ash as SCM. The 
reduction of unit weight caused by plastic waste addition is mainly due 
to the low density of the plastic waste compared to coarse aggregate it 
substituted because the replacement was done by volume [5]. The 
reduction of fresh unit weight due to fly ash is attributed to its low bulk 
density in comparison to cement it substituted as replacement method 
was carried out by volume [48]. On the contrary, GNP has very slight 
effect on the fresh unit weight of the concrete, as the percentage of the 
GNP added was very minimal. The slight increase in fresh unit weight is 
due to the filler effect of GNP and the increase in constituent materials 
content as the GNP was used as additive not as replacement material. 
4.2. Compressive strength 
Fig. 3 show the results of compressive strength for all ages of curing. 
The compressive strength increases with the curing age, which is 
attributed to continue hydration reaction of cement in the cementitious 
matrix. Mixes containing high fly ash with little or without GNP content 
show slower strength development with age, which causes significant 
reduction in strength. The compressive strength of mix P30F80G0.15 
decreases by 25.89%, 31.98%, and 32.66% at 3 days, 7 days, and 28 
days, respectively, in compared to the control mix. The reduction is 
mainly due to the slow pozzolanic hydration reactivity of fly ash at 
earlier ages, which consequently slows calcium-silicate-hydrate gels 
production, the major element for strength development [20]. 
Furthermore, small quantity of GNP in high volume of fly ash might not 
be very effective in enhancing and kindling the pozzolanic reactivity of 
fly ash at earlier age. However, the addition of GNP in higher quantity 
helps in enhancing the strength of the concrete containing HVFA. 
Comparing mix P0F40G0.3 (0.3% GNP) with P30F40G0 (no GNP), the 
strength of the former was significantly higher than that of the latter at 
all ages. Likewise, the strength of P0F40G0.15 (40% HVFA and 0.15% 
GNP) was higher than for the control mix by 27.15%, 13.33%, and 
6.17% at 3, 7, and 28 days, respectively. Similar findings have been 
reported by Li and Shi [50] where they utilized Graphene Oxide (GO) to 
improve strength development in fly ash based geopolymer concrete. 
This improvement can mainly be due to the ability of the graphene 
nanomaterials to consume more Quartz (SiO2), Mullite (Al2O3), and 
tricalcium aluminate and deplete more Portlandites (CH) thereby 
enhancing sufficient hydration reaction, thereby producing more C-S-H 
and C-A-S-H gels which results in higher strengths. Furthermore, GNP 
due to its electronegativity can act as a model for attracting the elec­
tropositive ions (Ca2+, Na+ and K+) in the matrix, the cations react with 
Al, Si and S to produce more hydration products including C-S-H, C-A-S- 
H and Ettringite (AFt). These hydration products densified the micro­
structure and improved strength development [50,51]. 
As clearly seen in the 3D plot of Fig. 4, using plastic waste aggregate 
as a partial substitution of coarse aggregate caused decline in 
compressive strength. Focusing on the mixes P30F40G0.15 and 
P0F40G0.15 having the same fly ash content (40%) and GNP content 
(0.15%), the compressive strength of P0F40G0.15 (without plastic 
aggregate) increases by 27.15%, 13.33%, and 6.17% at 3, 7, and 28 
days, respectively, comparing with the control mix, whereas the 
strength of P30F40G0.15 containing 30% of PW decreases by 14.12%, 
14.47%, and 16.71% at 3, 7, and 28 days, respectively, comparing with 
the control one. One of the reasons for strength reduction is poor bond 
between the plastic aggregate and the cement matrix due to the smooth 
texture of the plastic waste. Cracking and premature failure occurs 
through the weak bond with the application of load, thus leading to 
lower compressive strength [52,53]. Another factor is the hydrophobic 
nature of the plastic waste makes it entrap air and repel water in the 
fresh concrete, where the water easily forms a film around the plastic 
aggregate, thereby increasing pore volume in the hardened cementitious 
matrix [18,52,54]. Another factor is the lower stiffness and strength of 
the plastic waste compared to the natural coarse aggregate, triggering 
stress concentration within the cement matrix thereby enhancing dam­
age propagation and subsequently causing strength reduction [55]. The 
addition of GNP significantly improves the concrete’s strength. For some 
mixes with low fly ash and plastic waste contents, the GNP was suc­
cessfully in totally mitigating the undesirable effect of both the plastic 
waste and fly ash on the strength of the concrete. For example, the 
compressive strength of mix P30F0G0.15 which contains 0.15% GNP 
was higher by 7.25%, 19.28%, and 3.71% at 3, 7, and 28 days, respec­
tively, compared to the control even as 30% of natural coarse aggregate 
was replaced by plastic waste. For mix P15F20G0.225, the strength was 
more than that of the control by 58.63%, 27.64%, and 10.01% at 3, 7, 
and 28 days, respectively. Wang, et al. [24] reported similar findings 
that higher strength increment in concrete with GNP addition is due to 
enhancement of hydration reaction at early ages. 
4.3. Splitting tensile strength 
Fig. 5 present the results of splitting tensile strength for all curing 
ages. The splitting tensile strength increases with curing age due to 
continual cement hydration reaction during curing. Similar to the 
compressive strength, the splitting tensile strengths of mixes with high 
fly ash and low or no GNP contents have the lowest values, and their 
strength development with age was also slower. Mixtures with higher fly 
ash and low GNP content show significant reduction in strength at all 
ages. The splitting tensile strengths of mix P30F40G0 (30% PW and 40% 
HVFA) were lower by 34.14%, 27.95%, and 35.11% at 3, 7, and 28 days, 
respectively, compared to the control mix. The adverse effect of fly ash 
on splitting tensile strength of the concrete can be clearly seen by 
comparing the 3D surface plots presented in Fig. 6(a) – 6(c). The 
reduction in tensile strength with incorporation of high fly ash content is 
due to its weak and slow pozzolanic hydration reactivity which leads to 
less C-S-H gel and more Portlandites and Ettringites generation in the 
matrix, thereby increasing pore volume and consequently early pre­
mature cracking with load application leading to reduced tensile 
strength [20]. Furthermore, the tensile strength also declines with in­
crease plastic waste content as substitute to coarse aggregate as shown in 
Fig. 6(a) – 6(c). The decrease in splitting tensile strength was more 
noticeable in the mix containing higher fly ash content. This is due to the 
reasons stated above. 
Furthermore, mixes containing much quantity of plastic waste and 
lower or no GNP content also exhibit a higher reduction in tensile 
strength. From Fig. 5, the splitting tensile strength of P45F60G0.075 was 
lower by 6.42%, 29.85%, and 39.23% at 3, 7, and 28 days, respectively, 
in comparison to the control mix. This reduction is due to weak bond 
between the cementitious matrix and the plastic waste aggregate caused 
by its smooth surface, hydrophobic nature, and entrapment of free water 
on its surface, this results to premature crack initiation and consequently

4.4. Flexural strength 
The results of flexural strength for all ages of curing are presented in 
Fig. 7. Like the compressive and splitting tensile strengths, the flexural 
strength also increases with age of curing due to progression of hydra­
tion reaction. The flexural strength also decreases for mixes containing 
higher fly ash with little or no GNP content. The flexural strength of mix 
P30F80G0.15 was lower by 38.41% and 9.09% at 7 and 28 days in 
comparison to the control mix, while for mix P30F40G0 its flexural 
strength was less by 17.56% and 14.74% at 7 and 28 days, respectively, 
in comparison to the control mix. The reduction is flexural strength was 
more pronounced at 7 days which is caused by slower pozzolanic re­
action of fly ash at early age in combination with the negative effect of 
the plastic waste on the modulus of rupture of the concrete. Fly ash 
reduces the hydration products due to its very slow reactivity at early 
age which delayed strength progression and consequently leads to lower 
bending strength [57]. 
The use of higher plastic waste with lower or zero GNP content leads 
to the reduction in flexural strength. For instance, by comparing the 
flexural strength values of mixes P45F60G0.075, P60F40G0.15, 
P30F40G0.15, P45F20G0.225, and P45F20G0.075 with that of the 
control mix, the aforementioned mixes have lower flexural strength 
values at all ages. The reduction in flexural strength can be attributed 
the poor bond between the plastic waste and cement matrix which leads 
to formation of more weakness points and thereby causing premature 
failure under bending [3]. However, mixes with lower plastic waste and 
fly ash contents, plus higher GNP as additive showed better improve­
ment in flexural strength in comparison to the control. The highest 
flexural strength has been achieved in mix P15F20G0.075 where its 
flexural strength value was higher than that of the control by 21.08% 
and 27.41% at 7 and 28 days, respectively. The increment due to by 
plastic waste can be attributed to the fiber-like shape of the plastic waste 
aggregates, giving it more advantage of bridging cracking in the con­
crete compared to natural aggregate. This increases the bending resis­
tance and hence flexural strength [58].

concrete as the slope of the GNP portion was steeper, meaning that the 
flexural strength rises with increment in the GNP content. The highest 
flexural strength which was shown by the red portion was obtained at 
the regions of higher GNP content and lower plastic waste content. This 
can be worth mentioning that addition of GNP mitigated the negative 
effects of both plastic waste and fly ash on the bending strength of

4.5. Water absorption 
Fig. 9 presents the results of water absorption for the HVFA concrete 
mixes. Both fly ash and plastic waste escalate the water absorption of the 
concrete, thereby contributing negatively to its durability in terms 
porosity. Mixes containing a high combination of plastic waste and fly 
ash without or with little amount of GNP have the highest water ab­
sorption values. For instance, the water absorptions of mixes 
P45F60G0.075, P60F40G0.15, P45F60G0.225, P30F80G0.15, and 
P30F40G0 were higher by 58.51%, 35.24%, 29.42%, 48.58%, and 
46.90%, respectively, compared to the control concrete. The increment 
due to plastic waste addition is attributed to its hydrophobic nature 
which makes it entrap air during mixing, created more pores in the 
hardened cementitious matrix and consequently higher water absorp­
tion. Another reason is the enlargement of the ITZ between aggregate 
and cement paste caused by plastic waste, where this thickened ITZ 
absorbs more water [56]. While the increase in water absorption due to 
fly ash is caused by the less hydration reaction because of lower cement 
content used and slower pozzolanic reaction of fly ash. This reduces the 
generation of C-S-H and C-S-A-H and more crystalline Portlandites and 
therefore the micro and nanopores in the cement matrix are not able to 
be filled up, thereby increasing porosity and consequently water ab­
sorption [60,61]. According to the experimental results, plastic waste 
has more negative effect on the water absorption of the concrete 
compared to fly ash, this is because fly ash itself is a very fine powder, 
therefore even in unreacted form, it can act as a filler in the cementitious 
matrix [62]. 
The addition of GNP improved and mitigated the negative effects of 
both fly ash and plastic waste on water absorption of concrete. The water 
absorption values of mixes P0F40G0.15, P30F40G0.3, P15F20G0.225, 
P45F20G0.225, P30F0G0.15, and P15F20G0.075 were lower by 20.9%, 
2.04%, 38.26%, 13.07%, 19.60%, and 25.35%, respectively compared 
to the control mix. This might be attributed to the pore refinement 
ability of GNP due to its finer sizes (nano size) and nucleation site effect, 
giving it the ability to enhance hydration reaction and produce more C- 
S-H gels, thereby increasing the compactness and densifying the con­
crete microstructure and consequently decreasing porosity and water 
absorption. [21,23]. Furthermore, as explained in the previous section 
of compressive strength results, GNP has the ability to hasten hydration 
of fly ash at earlier age thereby consuming more Quartz (SiO2), Mullite 
(Al2O3), and tricalcium aluminate and reducing the amount of Por­
tlandites (CH), thereby enhancing more sufficient hydration reaction, 
producing more C-S-H and C-A-S-H gels which densify the concrete 
microstructure and reduce porosity and water absorption capacity 
[50,51]. 
4.6. Microstructural evaluation 
The microstructural morphology of some selected HVFA concrete 
mixes containing different contents of plastic waste, fly ash, and GNP is 
presented in Fig. 10(a) – 10(h). For the control mix Fig. 10(a), genera­
tion of C-S-H gels is due to cement hydration reaction with less 
unreacted Ca(OH)2. Mixture containing plastic waste and fly ash 
without any GNP such as mix P30F40G0 (Fig. 10(h)) has higher pores 
with many traces of Ca(OH)2 and few C-S-H gel deposits. This is caused 
by the hydrophobic nature of plastic waste resulting to increased pore 
volume and slower pozzolanic reactivity of fly ash thereby reducing 
hydration reaction leading to lower C-S-H gels and higher Ca(OH)2. 
The addition of GNP densified and refined the pores in the hardened 
concrete. For mix P30F0G0.15 which contains only 30% of plastic waste 
and 0.15% GNP without fly ash, the microstructural morphology (see 
Fig. 10(f)) was well refined, the ITZ between aggregate and paste was 
also densified, and there was no trace of Ca(OH)2 found. This was what 
led to mitigate the negative effect of plastic aggregate on strengths and 
water absorption of the concrete. This is because GNP serves as a 
nucleation site effect in the cement matrix thereby accelerating the 
hydration reaction in the concrete to produce more C-S-H gels, the major 
compound for the microstructure refinement [23]. Mixes containing 
high fly ash content with low GNP content such as mix P45F60G0.075 
(Fig. 10(c)) have many traces of ball shaped unreacted fly ash and 
unreacted Ca(OH)2 leading to a more porous matrix and ITZ. On the 
contrary, when higher GNP content was used in the mixture containing 
less or high fly ash content such as in mix P30F40G0.3 (Fig. 10(d)), there 
was very few or no traces of unreacted fly ash or Ca(OH)2, leading a 
more refined microstructure and a densified ITZ. This is due to the 
ability of GNP to consume more Quartz (SiO2), Mullite (Al2O3), and 
tricalcium aluminate and deplete more Portlandites (CH) thereby 
enhancing more sufficient hydration reaction and producing more C-S-H 
and Ettringite (AFt) [50,51]. Furthermore, less microcracks were seen in 
mixtures containing higher GNP content such as P30F40G0.3, 
P45F20G0.225, and P30F0G0.15, which is due to the GNP’s sheet-like 
nature that prevents microcrack deflection and inhibits the cracks 
from propagating [21,23]. For all the mixes containing GNP, no 
agglomeration of the GNP particles was seen in the microstructural 
morphology, and this is because GNP was properly dispersed in the 
mixture using a combination of mixing with superplasticizer and 
ultrasonication.

5.2. Multi-Objective optimization 
Mixture optimization has been carried out using multi-objective 
method to determine an optimum mix combination of the indepen­
dent variables by maximizing the strengths and minimizing the water 
absorption. A target criterion was set up so as all the possible combi­
nations for the variables and responses were captured. The variables 
were set within their ranges, i.e., PW ranging between 0% and 60%, 
HVFA ranging between 0% and 80%, and GNP ranging between 0% and 
0.3%. The multi-objective optimization was carried out using Design 
Expert Version 10, with the best desirability selected. 
Table 7 presents the results of optimization results. Several solutions 
were given by the RSM software, but the best six resolutions were 
selected based on highest desirability. The best optimized HVFA con­
crete containing plastic waste and GNP mix as selected by the software 
was achieved using first solution in Table 7. The optimum mix can be 
obtained by partial substitution of 15.3% coarse aggregate using plastic 
waste, 6.07% cement with fly ash, and adding 0.22% GNP by weight of 
binder materials to attain a compressive strength of 46.15 MPa, splitting 
tensile strength of 4.42 MPa, flexural strength of 9.45 MPa, and water 
absorption of 1.97%, with a desirability of 100%.

6. Conclusions 
The following conclusions were drawn:  
1) Plastic waste (PW) and fly ash improve the workability of high- 
volume fly ash (HVFA) concrete but decrease the fresh unit weight. 
While graphene nanoplatelets (GNP) reduces the workability and 
improves the fresh unit weight of HVFA concrete.  
2) Plastic waste and fly ash negatively affect the compressive, splitting 
tensile, and flexural strengths of HVFA concrete. A reduction in the 
28-day compressive, splitting tensile, and flexural strengths by up to 
30%, 35%, and 15%, respectively, were observed in the mixes con­
taining 30% PW and 40% fly ash without GNP.  
3) The addition of GNP has significantly enhanced and mitigated the 
negative effects of both plastic waste and fly ash on the strengths of 
HVFA concrete, and the effect is more effective at early ages. The 
addition of 0.225% GNP to HVFA mix containing 15% PW and 20% 
fly ash improves its compressive and splitting tensile strengths by up 
to 37% and 43.5%, respectively, at 3 days and 10% and 27%, 
respectively, at 28 days. Similarly, addition of 0.075% GNP improves 
the flexural strength of the mix containing 25% PW and 20% of fly 
ash by more than 21% and 27% at 7 and 28 days, respectively.  
4) Plastic waste and fly ash negatively affect the durability of HVFA 
concrete by increasing its water absorption, where up to 58.5%
