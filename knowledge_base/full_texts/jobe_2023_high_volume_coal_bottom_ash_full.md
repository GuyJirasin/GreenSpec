A B S T R A C T   
This study investigates the potential of using high-volume bottom ash as a supplementary 
cementitious material (SCM) and fine aggregate replacement in production of high-strength 
concrete. In the first stage, bottom ash quality was improved by sieving through a No. 50 sieve 
and grinding to increase fineness. Ground bottom ash (GBA) replaced ordinary Portland cement 
(OPC) at 35%, 50%, and 65% by weight to produce high-strength concrete. In the second stage, 
coarse bottom ash (CBA) retained by a No. 50 sieve replaced fine aggregate in an optimum 
mixture at 20%, 40%, and 60% by volume. Concrete properties including compressive strength, 
modulus of elasticity, autogenous shrinkage, and heat evolution were evaluated. The economic 
and environmental impacts of GBA were assessed. The results indicated that concrete with 50% 
GBA cement replacement could be classified as high-strength concrete at 7 d, achieving a 
compressive strength of approximately 80.9 MPa at 28 d. Autogenous shrinkage was decreased 
with an increase in GBA content. The heat evolution of concrete with 50% GBA was decreased by 
36.4% compared with the control concrete. Concrete with 50% GBA cement replacement and 
60% CBA fine aggregate replacement demonstrated the greatest reduction in autogenous 
shrinkage and peak of heat evolution, and it was classified as high-strength concrete.   
1. Introduction 
Coal combustion accounts for 27% of global energy production and 38% of the global electricity supply [1]. With increasing 
electricity demand, it is expected that coal combustion will account for 47% of the global electricity supply by 2030 [2]. Fly ash and 
bottom ash (BA) are the two main wastes of coal combustion in an electric power station. Fly ash is a smaller unburned airborne 
residual exiting the combustion zone that is collected by electro-precipitators in the chimneys. BA is a larger unburned residual that 
melts and precipitates at the bottom of the furnace; it constitutes approximately 15–25% of the total coal ash produced [1,3]. Disposing

Incineration of clay and limestone in cement production accounts for approximately 7% of anthropogenic carbon dioxide emissions 
[5]. Current trends in interdisciplinary research include promotion of sustainable development through production of “green con­
crete”, partially replacing cement with supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) such as fly-ash, BA, rice husk ash, and silica 
fume, or hydraulic minerals such as blast furnace slag. Although they have disadvantages, fly ash and BA are classified as pozzolanic 
materials. They can improve the long-term strength of concrete through a progressive pozzolanic reaction that occurs with devel­
opment of C–S–H [6,7]. Using these wastes in a concrete mixture minimizes landfill disposal and promotes cost reduction and sus­
tainable development by reducing the amount of cement produced and carbon dioxide emissions. In addition, SCMs improve concrete 
workability, mitigate bleeding, reduce permeability, and reduce heat from the hydration process [8]. 
Many studies have investigated the effects of BA as an aggregate substitute; BA has been scientifically proven to be a suitable 
material for fine aggregate replacement [9,10]. Kim and Lee [11] investigated the effects of BA aggregate on concrete properties and 
reported no significant effects on compressive strength development. Reductions in bending strength and elastic modulus were 
observed at higher replacement ratios. BA has been used as an internal curing additive to mitigate self-desiccation and shrinkage in 
cement mortar systems [12]. Bai et al. [13] reported that drying shrinkage was reduced when the content of BA as a fine aggregate 
replacement was optimized. The porosity in the BA structure functions as a water reservoir that allows moisture movement into the 
surrounding cement matrix. BA has been reported to enhance capillary water absorption, resulting in increased wetting time [14] and 
air content [15]. Dry BA aggregate is reported to decrease concrete workability as its porous structure absorbs free water from the mix 
and its irregular shape, rough surface texture, and ultra-high particle fineness (with grinding) reduce workability [10,11,16]. How­
ever, the reduced workability can be mitigated with use of saturated surface-dry BA [9]. Singh and Siddique [17] reported that the 
abrasion resistance of concrete increased when coal BA was used as a fine aggregate replacement. Several studies have proposed that 
use of coal BA as a fine aggregate replacement reduces dead weight of a concrete structure because BA has a lower specific gravity than 
natural sand [11,16,18]. 
The effects of fly ash as an SCM have been extensively investigated. According to ASTM C618 [19], the specifications and specific 
properties of fly ash and other pozzolan for concrete works, criterions to evaluate good pozzolanic materials are renowned. BA contains 
approximately 70–90% silica, alumina, and iron, and can be classified as a pozzolanic material [1]. Thus, BA can potentially be used as 
a constitute binder in concrete. To the best knowledge of the authors, few studies have attempted to improve BA quality using a 
grinding process to increase material fineness. It has been reported that an increase in BA fineness significantly enhances pozzolanic 
reactivity [20] and promotes the pore-filling effect [3], increasing the packing density of the binder [21,22]. Kim [23] reported that the 
compressive strength of specimens with cut-size BA at 60 d was improved by 20–30% compared to control specimens. The grinding 
process and sieving of BA produced noticeable improvement in the degree of hydration and pozzolanic reaction, improving work­
ability. Oruji et al. [24] suggested that an increase in the fineness of BA particles greatly improves the strength activity and micro­
structure of cement mortar. In other words, it improves cement replacement tolerances, with negligible effects on compressive strength 
development. In addition, thermal treatment can be used to improve BA quality to decrease loss on ignition, decompose calcium 
carbonate [25], and improve pozzolanic reactivity [24,26]. 
Generally, a high binder content and low water-to-binder (W/B) ratio are required to produce high-strength concrete, with several 
limitations. The heat evolution from cement hydration is considerably high, potentially producing thermal crack development due to 
an imbalance in thermal expansion caused by the difference in temperature gradient between the surface and the inner concrete core. 
In addition, concrete with a low W/B ratio is prone to cracking under restraint caused by autogenous shrinkage, as there is insufficient 
water to complete cement hydration [27]. Large capillary stresses are produced due to self-desiccation of cement paste, causing rapid 
autogenous shrinkage [28]. Schr¨ofl et al. [27] further suggested that the degree of self-desiccation is associated with two main 
mechanisms: reduced disjoining pressure in C–S–H gels and reduced meniscus radii in the pore water, producing tension in the pore 
water and at its surface. As concrete enters the hardening stage, compressive stress develops to restore equilibrium by counteracting 
the tensile stresses in the fluid phase, reducing the volume of the entire cement matrix. Sieved BA and ground coal bottom ash (GBA) 
can potentially be used to produce high-strength concrete, as their powders can be distributed in the cement matrix, producing a filler 
effect [29]. Khongpermgoson et al. [21] investigated the mechanical properties of high-strength concrete using high-fineness BA as a 
pozzolanic binder. The results indicated that partial replacement of ordinary Portland cement (OPC) with GBA at 15–45 wt% produced 
compressive strength greater than 55 MPa, indicating high-strength concrete. Use of GBA as a pozzolanic binder significantly reduces 
heat from hydration. Thus, GBA is thought to be a promising material for producing low-heat high-strength concrete. Several studies 
have reported that GBA can be used as an internal curing additive as it has a high-water retention capacity, associated with its porous 
structure [12,30–32]. As a result, autogenous shrinkage is mitigated as the internal moisture supply is extended. 
Fly ash is one of the most reutilized coal combustion wastes. According to Pormmoon et al. [3], the demand for fly ash in normal 
and special concrete production has sharply increased, causing a four-fold price increase; much smaller amounts of BA are being 
reutilized [33]. With the demand for fly ash outpacing the supply, additional paradigms for BA use as a concrete additive are necessary 
to promote reutilization and minimize landfilled waste. 
Based on a previous study, this study further investigates the possibility of producing high-strength concrete using high-volume 
GBA as an SCM and fine aggregate replacement. First, the GBA quality was improved by sieving and grinding before use as a 
replacement for OPC at 35%, 50%, and 65% by weight. Next, the GBA retained on a No. 50 sieve replaced the fine aggregate in 
combination with the GBA binder. The sand replacement ratios were 20%, 40%, and 60% by volume. The evaluation criteria included 
rheological behavior, compressive strength development, modulus of elasticity, autogenous shrinkage, and heat evolution. The op­
timum GBA percentages for cement and sand replacement to produce high-strength concrete were determined based on the

2. Experimental program 
2.1. Materials 
The materials used in this experiment were OPC, fine aggregate (natural river sand), coarse aggregate, GBA, and type F 
superplasticizer. 
2.1.1. Cement and BA 
OPC used in this study was classified as type I according to TIS 15 [34]. After sieving, approximately 11.35% of the mass was 
retained on a No. 325 sieve. GBA used in this study was obtained from combustion of lignite coal at the Mae Moh power plant, Lampang 
Province, Thailand. The specific gravities of OPC and GBA were 3.14 and 2.86, respectively. The chemical compositions of OPC and 
GBA are presented in Table 1. The chemical composition of GBA included SiO2, Al2O3, and Fe2O3 contents of 25.3%, 12.8%, and 
22.0%, respectively, similar to the chemical composition of fly ash (FA) obtained from the same source. These three main components 
accounted for 60.1% of the total. This GBA is classified as a class C pozzolanic material according to ASTM C618 [19]. 
2.1.2. Aggregates 
Washed river sand passing through a No. 4 sieve was used as the fine aggregate. Its specific gravity, water absorption, and fineness 
modulus were 2.65, 0.91%, and 2.96, respectively. Coarse aggregate was prepared by sieving through a No. 3/8 sieve. The coarse 
aggregate specific gravity, water absorption, and fineness modulus were 2.71, 0.51%, and 6.74, respectively. 
2.1.3. Superplasticizer 
Type F superplasticizer (SP) was used to control the slump between 150 mm and 200 mm. A slump test was conducted in 
accordance with ASTM C143 [35]. 
2.2. Preparation of BA 
BA was processed for substitution of two concrete materials, as a cement replacement and as a fine aggregate replacement. As- 
received BA was oven-dried at 110 ± 5 ◦C for 24 h to eliminate moisture. For use as a cement replacement, the BA quality was 
improved by sieving through a No. 50 sieve and ball-mill grinding until the particles retained on a No. 325 sieve were less than 1% by 
weight. This method was suggested by Pormmoon et al. [3], who reported that sieving BA to smaller sizes before grinding significantly 
improved the ground BA quality in terms of compressive strength. A summary of the preparation processes is presented in Fig. 1. 
To investigate the influence of GBA on the strength activity index (SAI), OPC mortar samples were prepared for reference, and BA 
without sieving through a No. 50 sieve before grinding (GBA-O) and BA with sieving through a No. 50 sieve before grinding (GBA-P) 
were used as cement replacement at 20 wt%. The picture of GBA-O and GBA-P are illustrated in Fig. 1. Mortar flow was controlled at 
110 ± 5%, with W/B ratios of 0.61, 0.63, and 0.62 for OPC, GBA-O, and GBA-P, respectively. The binder-to-sand ratio was constant at 
2.75. Mortar cubes with dimensions of 50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm were cast to determine the compressive strength at 7 d and 28 
d according to ASTM C109 [36]. Table 2 shows the SAI of OPC, GBA-O, and GBA-P. GBA-P had a higher pozzolanic reactivity than 
GBA-O at all testing ages. The SAIs of GBA-P and GBA-O at 7 d and 28 d were 101% and 113%, and 92% and 101%, respectively. GBA-P 
was used as a cement replacement to produce high-strength concrete throughout this study. In addition, BA retained on a No. 50 sieve 
was used as a fine-aggregate replacement (CBA) in an optimum mixture using GBA-P as a cement replacement. The gradations of the 
fine aggregate and CBA are compared in Fig. 2.

Four mixes (CT, GBA35, GBA50, and GBA65) were used to determine the optimum GBA replacement ratio to achieve the best concrete 
properties. The control specimen (CT) contained a binder of 100% OPC. Cement was replaced by GBA at 35%, 50%, and 65% by weight

2.4.2. Autogenous shrinkage test 
Autogenous shrinkage was measured in accordance with ASTM C490 [40] using rectangular prisms with dimensions of 75 × 75 ×

50%. The average autogenous shrinkage of the concrete was determined from three specimens over a period of 1–60 d. 
2.4.3. Heat evolution 
The heat generated from cement hydration and pozzolanic reaction was investigated. The cubic mold of 450 × 450 × 450 mm3 with 
50-mm thick insulator placed inside all surfaces was prepared. Then, the concrete was cast inside this mold producing 350 × 350 ×
350 mm3 cubic specimen. The insulator was provided to prevent heat transfer during the monitoring period. Thermocouples were 
implanted in the center core of the specimens to monitor heat evolution. After casting, the thermocouples were connected to a data 
logger. The monitoring period was divided into five intervals: 0–12 h, 12–24 h, 24–48 h, 48–72 h, and 72–168 h after casting; 
measurements were recorded every 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, and 12 h, respectively, in each interval. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Superplasticizer requirement 
The W/B ratio was fixed at 0.25 and the slump was controlled from 150 to 200 mm. The slump test was conducted in accordance 
with ASTM C143 [35]. The SP was used to improve rheological properties and concrete workability in a fresh state by promoting 
dispersion of cement particles [41–43]. The SP contents were varied to control the slump without changing the W/B ratio. The 
improved GBA quality as a cement replacement produced a lower weight retained on sieve No. 325 than with cement, suggesting that 
GBA contains finer particles than cement. With its porous structure, GBA potentially absorbs water from the mix. In combination, these 
effects theoretically produce a reduction in slump. 
In Table 3, it is observed that specimens with a GBA binder required more SP than CT specimens to control slump. In addition, SP 
content needed to be increased with increases in GBA replacement ratio. SP was adopted in range of 5.0–9.5 kg/m3 (0.9–1.7% weight 
of the binder). Fig. 3a shows that the CT specimen required an SP content of 5 kg/m3 to achieve a slump of 150 mm. GBA35, GBA50, 
and GBA65 required SP contents of 5.6 kg/m3, 6.2 kg/m3, and 6.7 kg/m3 to achieve slumps of 170 mm, 160 mm, and 180 mm, 
respectively.

3.2. Compressive strength 
The compressive strength results at 7 d, 14 d, 28 d, and 90 d for specimens with GBA binder are presented in Fig. 4. The CTs had 
compressive strengths of 61.4 MPa, 67.5 MPa, 72.1 MPa, and 74.2 MPa at 7 d, 14 d, 28 d, and 90 d of curing, respectively. GBA35 had 
compressive strengths of 58.0 MPa, 66.7 MPa, 75.3 MPa, and 78.4 MPa, respectively. GBA35 had a lower 7-d compressive strength 
than CT; however, its strength increase rate was greater after 14 d of curing, with a greater final strength than CT, consistent with the 
study of Chandra and Bjornstrom [43], who proposed that C–S–H formation from cement hydration contributes to strength gain faster 
than C–S–H formation due to the reaction between pozzolan and portlandite (pozzolanic reaction) at an early age. Strength devel­
opment due to pozzolanic reaction is observed at a later age. 
With 50% GBA cement replacement, GBA50 had compressive strengths of 63.2 MPa, 72.5 MPa, 80.9 MPa, and 84.5 MPa at 7 d, 14 
d, 28 d, and 90 d, respectively, approximately 103%, 107%, 112%, and 114% greater than the compressive strength of CT. According 
to ACI 363R [44], GBA50 is classified as high-strength concrete after 7 d of curing as the compressive strength is higher than 55 MPa. 
The significant increase in compressive strength developed from an early age is attributed to the effect of GBA. Although it has been 
reported that pre-treated BA decreased the strength of the concrete due to its porous structure [4,16], strength activity index of 
post-treated BA (GBA) has shown that sieving BA to smaller sizes before grinding significantly improved the ground BA quality in terms 
of compressive strength as shown in Section 2.2, which agrees well the earlier study [3]. In addition, GBA (BA with decrease in particle 
size) increases concrete compressive strength through a combination of the filling effect and an increase in particle fineness, enhancing 
the reactivity of pozzolanic reaction which is well supported by the work of Kim [23]. The filling effect of GBA is clearly observed in 
case GBA50 as its compressive strength is improved compared to CT series at 7 days of curing. Meanwhile, the effect of pozzolanic 
reaction on strength development is well observed in case GBA35 and GBA50 as their strength surpass CT series after 14 days of curing. 
Also, Pormmoon et al. [3] reported that an increase in fineness improves amorphous content in GBA, and that improving GBA quality 
by sieving before grinding potentially amplifies the amorphous content in GBA. 
GBA65 had compressive strengths of 50.1 MPa, 59.2 MPa, 66.2 MPa, and 70.2 MPa at 7 d, 14 d, 28 d, and 90 d, respectively, with 
lower initial and final strengths than those of the CT series. For GBA65, more than half of the total binder (65%) was replaced with 
GBA; the decrease in strength may have been triggered by insufficient cement content, altering the hydration reaction and producing 
less portlandite. As the pozzolanic reaction is controlled by the amount of portlandite, a product of cement hydration, the amount of 
C–S–H generated from cement hydration and the pozzolanic reaction was reduced. However, GBA65 was classified as high-strength 
concrete after 14 d of curing, theoretically allowing cement manufacturers to produce high-strength concrete with lower cost and

GBA50. The greatest reduction in compressive strength was found in GBA50-60S at 7 d of curing, an almost 20% decrease in strength 
compared to GBA50. The CBA used in this experiment had lower material toughness than natural sand. Thus, it lowered compressive 
strength, especially with a high CBA replacement ratio. It seems that pozzolanic reaction is not promoted when a high CBA ratio is 
used. The long-term strength only slightly increased from the initial strength, possibly due to a shortage of portlandite, the control 
reactant that vitalizes the pozzolanic reaction. 
GBA50-20S had higher compressive strengths than the CT series at all ages; GBA50-40S and GBA50-60S exhibited a negligible 
reduction in compressive strength at 90 d compared to the CT series, confirming that improved GBA and CBA quality in cement and 
sand replacements can produce high-strength concrete. 
GBA35, GBA50, and GBA65 can be used to produce high-strength concrete. As the BA reutilization rate is high, less BA must be sent 
to landfills. Reduced OPC content in high-strength concrete reduces its cost and the carbon dioxide emitted in the manufacturing 
process. In combination, these approaches promote the UN sustainable development goals and a more sustainable future for the 
construction industry. 
3.3. Modulus of elasticity 
The modulus of elasticity (Ec) of specimens with GBA binder measured at 28 d and 90 d is presented in Fig. 5. The average Ec values 
were determined from three specimens. GBA35 and GBA50 had an average Ec at 28 d of 45.4 GPa and 48.7 GPa, respectively, 
compared with 43.7 GPa for the CT series; GBA65 had the lowest average Ec (39.1 GPa). At 90 d, the average Ec of CT, GBA35, GBA50, 
and GBA65 were 45.2 GPa, 47.6 GPa, 49.9 GPa, and 40.5 GPa, respectively. The average Ec increased with time for all series and 
exhibited a direct correlation to the trend of compressive strength development; however, the presence of GBA indicated no distinctive 
effect on Ec development at 90 d. GBA35 and GBA50 had a higher average Ec than the CT series; GBA65 had the lowest Ec value. 
Although modulus of elasticity increases as the compressive strength of the concrete increases, use of GBA in concrete has no direct 
correlation to modulus of elasticity as it increases the strength of the cement paste, whereas the Ec value is the result of strength and 
deformability of the cement paste and aggregate, including the bonding properties at the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) in concrete 
[45]. This is consistent with the work of Neville [46], who proposed that aggregate strength has a greater influence on the modulus of 
elasticity than the strength of the cement paste. 
For the group containing GBA and CBA as cement and sand replacement, respectively, GBA50-20S, GBA50-40S, and GBA50-60S 
had an average Ec of 44.3 GPa, 39.2 GPa, and 37.3 GPa, respectively, at 28 d. The average Ec of these specimens at 90 d was 46.0 
GPa, 40.9 GPa, and 40.3 GPa, respectively. Although an increase in Ec was observed in all series in this group at 90 d compared to 28 d, 
the average Ec values of all specimens in this group at 28 d and 90 d were less than that of GBA50. A decrease in Ec values was observed

3.4. Autogenous shrinkage 
Fig. 6 shows the autogenous shrinkage of samples measured at 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, 14 d, 28 d, and 90 d with different contents of GBA as a 
cement replacement. Autogenous shrinkage rapidly increased within the first 14 d, and gradually increased after 28 d, similar to the 
trend for strength development. It is suggested that the rate of autogenous shrinkage is directly related to the rate of cement hydration 
as autogenous shrinkage is caused by a loss of water in cement hydration, inducing negative capillary pressure inside the cement 
matrix, causing the concrete to shrink. Subsequently, a crack can be formed when restrain is presented [47]. Concrete with a low W/B 
ratio is prone to cracking, as cement hydration rapidly consumes water at an early age. Generally, high-strength concrete is designed 
with a low W/B ratio to achieve satisfactory compressive strength. This limitation potentially intensifies autogenous shrinkage, 
particularly when higher strength is required. In this study, the W/B ratio was fixed at 0.25 to investigate the effect of improved GBA 
binder quality on autogenous shrinkage. The CT series had autogenous shrinkage values of 111 × 10−6 mm/mm, 232 × 10−6 mm/mm, 
337 × 10−6 mm/mm, 423 × 10−6 mm/mm, 495 × 10−6 mm/mm, and 515 × 10−6 mm/mm at 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, 14 d, 28 d, and 90 d, 
respectively, the highest values of all series. The autogenous shrinkage of GBA65 at 90 d was nearly 40% less than that of CT, sug­
gesting that replacement of cement with GBA significantly slows cement hydration and mitigates autogenous shrinkage. Autogenous 
shrinkage gradually decreases as the GBA replacement ratio is increased. 
Fig. 7 shows the autogenous shrinkage measured at 1 d, 3 d, 7 d, 14 d, 28 d, and 90 d with different CBA contents. All specimens in 
this group exhibited less autogenous shrinkage than GBA50 at all measurement times. The autogenous shrinkage decreased as the CBA 
replacement ratio was increased. GBA50-60S had the lowest autogenous shrinkage, 51 × 10−6 mm/mm, 93 × 10−6 mm/mm, 141 ×
10−6 mm/mm, 181 × 10−6 mm/mm, 216 × 10−6 mm/mm, and 230 × 10−6 mm/mm, respectively, approximately 60% less than that 
of CT at all measurement times. Use of GBA as a cement replacement significantly reduced autogenous shrinkage; autogenous 
shrinkage was further mitigated using both GBA and CBA. GBA and CBA produce an internal curing effect due to high porosity in their 
microstructures [12,30–32]. Thus, they are prone to absorb water, potentially acting as small water reservoirs inside the cement matrix 
that release moisture locally and compensate for the water loss from cement paste in the hydration process [48–50]. 
3.5. Heat evolution 
High-strength concrete is usually designed with a low W/B ratio and high cement content to achieve satisfactory compressive 
strength. However, the likelihood of thermal crack formation can be high due to an imbalance in thermal expansion caused by the heat 
evolution from cement hydration. Heat evolution from cement hydration of CT, GBA50, and GBA50-60S were compared; the tem­
perature increases are summarized in Fig. 8 and Table 4. The temperatures increased sharply approximately 10 h after casting; the rate 
of increase decreased approximately 16 h after casting for all series. The specimens reached room temperature at approximately 144 h. 
CT exhibited the greatest temperature increase (44 ◦C), approximately 16 h after casting. With its high OPC content, heat was 
generated through hydration of C3S, C2S, C3A, and C4AF [51]. Mixtures with high cement content are expected to exhibit greater heat 
evolution due to the primary hydration reaction [21,52,53]. 
GBA50 (cement content reduced by 50%) exhibited moderate temperature increases compared to the other series, with a maximum 
temperature increase of 28 ◦C at approximately 22 h, 1.6 times less than that of CT. The maximum heat evolution was reduced by 
36.4% and the peak temperature was delayed by 5 h with 50% GBA as a cement replacement. GBA50-60S exhibited a maximum 
temperature increase of 24 ◦C, less than that of the other series and 1.8 times less than that of CT. In GBA50-60S, although, CBA 
replacement had less influence on heat evolution and peak temperature retardation than GBA replacement, an approximately 45% 
reduction in temperature increase and a 7-h delay in peak temperature were observed with GBA and CBA replacement compared to CT. 
It can be seen that in addition to the effect of pozzolanic materials (GBA) to reduce the heat evolution, the CBA further mitigated the

3.6. Environmental and economic impacts 
Use of BA is currently restricted to mostly road base substitution or cement feed stock applications [24]. The remaining BA is sent to 
landfills without being value-added or beneficially reutilized. Dumping it into landfills without proper treatment pollutes the soil 
which potentially harms biodiversity and the environment. According to the Electricity Generating Authority of Thailand (EGAT), the 
cost of BA disposal was approximately 3.2 USD per ton in fiscal year 2018 [33]. The amount of BA produced at the Mae Moh power 
plant in Thailand has been estimated as approximately 1.4 × 106 ton/year [33]. Thus, an estimated annual budget of 4.5 million USD is 
required for BA disposal. With a shortage of fly ash and increasing demand for SCMs in concrete production, use of BA seems to be a 
reasonable solution. 
Cement manufacturing is recognized as an industry that is not ecofriendly; approximately 4.7 million BTU of energy are consumed 
per ton of cement produced, comparable to approximately 400 pounds of coal, emitting approximately one ton of CO2 [54] from 
incineration of limestone and clay at a temperature of 1500 ◦C [55]. It has been estimated that CO2 emissions associated with the 
grinding process are approximately 0.1 ton per ton of cement produced; incineration of limestone (decarbonation) and carbon-based 
fuels used for heating the clinker produces approximately 0.53 tons and 0.39 tons of CO2, respectively, per ton of cement produced [56, 
57]. Thus, the two main ways to reduce CO2 emissions are to reduce clinker production and to reduce cement content in concrete. 
This study demonstrates that high-strength concrete with compressive strength values of 70–85 MPa is possible with partial cement 
replacement with GBA. To achieve such high compressive strength, the conventional mix proportion requires replacing cement with 
silica fume up to 10–15% by weight of the binder [58,59]. Silica fume can cost up to 800 USD/ton. Meanwhile, the cost of GBA is 
estimated approximately 3.4 USD/ton which is about 200 and 19 times more cost-effective compared to silica fume and cement, 
respectively. Furthermore, a higher risk of respiratory illnesses such silicosis, tuberculosis, chronic bronchitis, chronic obstructive 
pulmonary disease (COPD) is also reported to be linked to occupational exposure to silica fume [60]. With these limitations, the use of 
alternative binder to produce high strength concrete has become more interesting. GBA35 and GBA50 exhibit better mechanical 
properties than the CT series and mitigate the risk of cracking caused by autogenous shrinkage and thermal expansion, the main 
difficulties with high-strength concrete as they require costly and labor-intensive repair. Concrete with a GBA binder can potentially 
reduce maintenance costs. Grinding of BA to a necessary particle size consumes less energy and releases less CO2 than clinker; grinding 
of BA is expected to be more cost-effective and environmentally friendly [33,56]. GBA reutilization rates of 35% and 50% by binder 
weight can reduce the amount of cement necessary in concrete production and the amount of waste sent to landfills. GBA65 had a 
compressive strength of 70.2 MPa at 90 d, an approximately 5% decrease in final strength compared to that of the CT series; however, 
the GBA reutilization rate of 65% reduced CO2 emissions by more than 58.5% compared to the emissions of the CT series. 
Table 5 presents a cost analysis of OPC concrete and BA concrete. The average cost of OPC (type I) is estimated as approximately 
64.7 USD per ton; the cost of GBA is primarily related to transportation and grinding to achieve the necessary fineness, estimated as 
approximately 3.4 USD per ton. The total cost of the CT is calculated as 68.3 USD/m3, the greatest cost of all series. The cost of BA 
concrete is the lowest; the total cost of GBA65 is approximately 47.4 USD/m3. The cost of high-strength concrete can be reduced by up 
to 30% when 65% of the cement is replaced with GBA. Considering the cost of concrete per compressive strength (unit cost), GBA50 
has the lowest cost (0.62 USD/MPa), mainly because it has the highest compressive strength. Although using CBA in GBA50 decreased 
compressive strength at all CBA replacement ratios, all GBA–CBA specimens achieved satisfactory compressive strength. As BA is a 
waste, CBA’s cost is included in the grinding cost of GBA, considered as 0 USD per ton. As the cost of CBA is less than the cost of fine 
aggregate, GBA50-40 and GBA50-60 are more economical than GBA50 because a considerable amount of fine aggregate is replaced 
with CBA. 
Use of BA as a replacement for cement and sand in high-strength concrete improves the mechanical properties and reduces 
maintenance costs from cracking caused by autogenous shrinkage and thermal expansion, and promotes reutilization of BA, reducing 
the cement requirement in the mix proportion, the amount of waste disposed in landfills, and CO2 emissions in manufacturing. This 
interdisciplinary research has substantial economic and environmental impacts. Improved BA is promising for use as an SCM and sand 
replacement in production of green high-strength concrete. 
4. Conclusions 
This research embraces the UN sustainable development goals, producing green high-strength concrete using improved GBA and 
CBA as partial replacements for cement and natural fine aggregate, respectively. High-strength concrete properties including rheo­
logical behavior, compressive strength, modulus of elasticity, autogenous shrinkage, and heat evolution were evaluated. The exper­
imental program included a feasibility assessment of high-strength concrete made with GBA and CBA, including economic and 
environmental impacts. The following conclusions can be drawn:  
1. Specimens with 50% cement replacement with GBA had the highest compressive strength at 28 d (80.9 MPa), the highest modulus 
of elasticity, and were classified as high-strength concrete at 7 d. The increased compressive strength is due to a combination of the 
pore filling effect of GBA and the progressive pozzolanic reaction. Specimens with 65% cement replacement with GBA had the 
lowest compressive strength. The decrease in compressive strength was found to be associated with insufficient cement content that

altered the hydration reaction, producing less portlandite for pozzolanic reaction. Specimens with 35% and 65% GBA could be 
classified as high-strength concrete at 28 d.  
2. Use of CBA slightly reduced compressive strength and elastic modulus because CBA has a lower material strength than natural sand. 
Nevertheless, specimens with GBA and CBA met the high-strength concrete criteria at 28 d.  
3. Autogenous shrinkage was reduced with increasing GBA and CBA replacement ratios. Autogenous shrinkage at 90 d with 50% GBA 
as binder and 60% CBA as natural sand replacement was reduced by 55.3% compared with the control specimen due to the internal 
curing effect of BA and lower heat evolution caused by pozzolanic reaction. As BA has a porous structure, it is prone to absorb 
water, potentially acting as a small reservoir distributing water inside the cement matrix. The absorbed water is released locally to 
compensate for the water loss from cement paste in the hydration process. In addition, autogenous shrinkage is greatly reduced due 
to lower heat evolution caused by pozzolanic reaction.  
4. With GBA cement replacement, the heat evolution decreases proportionally to the decrease in cement content. CBA has some 
influence in heat evolution mitigation as its porous structure enhances heat transfer through its large surface area and internal 
water storage capability. An increase in the CBA replacement ratio has less impact in reducing heat evolution than replacing cement 
with GBA; however, the peak temperature is further reduced in GBA–CBA mixtures.  
5. Use of BA as a cement and sand replacement in high-strength concrete greatly enhances mechanical properties and reduces the risk 
of cracking due to autogenous shrinkage and thermal expansion. It allows reduced CO2 emissions in concrete manufacture through 
increased reutilization of BA and reduced cement content in concrete. These approaches have substantial economic and envi­
ronmental impacts. Improved BA is a promising material for use as an SCM and sand replacement in production of green high- 
strength concrete.
