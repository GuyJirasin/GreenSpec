Compressive Behavior and Durability Performance of
High-Volume Fly-Ash Concrete with Plastic
Waste and Graphene Nanoplatelets by
Using Response-Surface Methodology
Musa Adamu1; Pattanawit Trabanpruek2; Varuj Limwibul3; Pitcha Jongvivatsakul4;
Mitsuyasu Iwanami5; and Suched Likitlersuang6
Abstract: Plastic waste (PW) generation continuously increases every year due to the growing population and demand for plastic materials.
This situation poses a challenge to many countries, including developed ones, on how to dispose of PW. Accordingly, PW was utilized in this
study to replace coarse aggregates partially in high-volume fly-ash (HVFA) concrete. However, PW decreased the strength and durability of
concrete. To address this issue, graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs) were added to mitigate the negative consequences of PW and HVFA on
concrete’s properties. The objective of this study is to investigate the influences of PWand GNP contents on the durability and deformation of
HVFA concrete. Response-surface methodology (RSM) was used to design and optimize a series of cement mixes to achieve the most
desirable properties. Independent variables included PW content as partial replacement for coarse aggregates (0%, 15%, 30%, 45%, and
60% by volume), fly ash as partial substitute for cement (0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and 80% by volume), and GNPs as additives (0%, 0.075%,
0.15%, 0.225%, and 0.3%). The considered responses were concrete unit weight, modulus of elasticity (MoE), and Cantabro abrasion loss
at 300 revolutions. Results showed that PW and HVFA decreased concrete unit weight and MoE but increased Cantabro abrasion loss.
By contrast, GNPs increased concrete unit weight and MoE but decreased Cantabro abrasion loss. PW and HVFA also increased the com-
pressive toughness and porosity of concrete, while GNPs increased its stiffness but decreased its compressive toughness and porosity. The
mathematical models developed to predict the unit weight, MoE, and abrasion resistance of concrete were significant, with errors of less than
6%. An optimized mix was achieved by partially replacing 12.44% of coarse aggregates with PW and 24.57% of cement with fly ash and
adding 0.279% GNPs with a desirability of 100%. DOI: 10.1061/(ASCE)MT.1943-5533.0004377. © 2022 American Society of Civil
Engineers.

Introduction
Plastic materials ranging from bottles, nylon, and electrical appli-
ances have become basic and necessary parts of human life. They
are used in homes, schools, hospitals, markets, and other places.
Plastics are preferred over other materials with similar functions
due to their low cost, low mass, durability, and simplicity of use
(Misri et al. 2018; Saxena et al. 2018). Consequently, the amount
of plastic waste (PW) that is generated and must be disposed of
continues to increase annually. One of the most effective ap-
proaches for managing PW is repurposing it into building and con-
struction materials, such as reusing PW as a partial substitute for
natural aggregates in concrete and pavement (Misri et al. 2018;
Bahij et al. 2020).
In recent decades, PW has been utilized as a partial substitute
for coarse and fine aggregates in concrete. Adnan and Dawood
(2021) investigated the effects of partially replacing fine aggregates
with plastic box waste particles (PBWPs) on the mechanical prop-
erties of concrete and the flexural performance of reinforced con-
crete (RC) beams. Their findings showed that partially replacing
natural aggregates with PBWPs enhances the compressive and
flexural strengths of concrete but decreases its tensile strength.
In addition, PBWPs enhance the ultimate failure load, ductility,
and cracking strength of RC beams. Abu-Saleem et al. (2021) used

(MoE) decrease with increments in all types of PW, with PETyield-

PW has also been used in other construction material applica-
tions, such as pavements. Kamarudin et al. (2021) added treated
PW (TPW) to asphalt at 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75%, 1%, 2%, and 3%
by weight of the total mix to improve the properties of the asphalt
mix. Their findings indicated that the addition of TPW improves
the stiffness, stability, fatigue cracking, rutting, and stripping re-
sistance of the mix. The optimum dosage of TPW was determined
to be 0.75%, which enhanced fatigue cracking, rutting, and strip-
ping resistance by 100%, 73%, and 25%, respectively. Abdullah
et al. (2017b) investigated the effect of PW addition on the rheo-
logical properties of bitumen by adding PW at 1.5%, 3.0%, 4.5%,
and 6.0% by weight of bitumen. They reported a reduction in the
penetration number and an increase in the softening point and vis-
cosity of bitumen at 135°C with an increase in PW content. This
finding implied that PW improves the rutting performance of bitu-
men. Abd Kader et al. (2017) evaluated the effect of PWaddition on
the optimum binder content of asphalt in terms of marshal stability,
flow, density, total air voids in the mix, voids in the aggregates, and
voids filled with bitumen. They added different proportions of PW,
i.e., 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% by weight of the binder. Their findings
showed that the addition of PW increases the optimum binder
content of the asphalt mixes, with an optimum dosage of 8% PW.
By contrast, PW addition decreases the volumetric properties of the
asphalt mixes. Abdullah et al. (2017a) also investigated the effect of
PW addition as a binder modifier on the properties of asphalt mix.
PW was added at 4%, 6%, 8%, and 10% by weight of bitumen.
Their results indicated that the addition of PW improved the proper-
ties of the asphalt, including its stability, tensile strength, resilient
modulus, and creep modulus. The optimum dosage of PW was
8% in terms of tensile strength (1,049 kPa), resilient modulus
(3,422 MPa at 25°C and 494 MPa at 40°C), and creep modulus
(73.3 MPa). With regard to stability, asphalt with 4% PW achieved
the highest value of 184 kN.
Although PW has been reported to exert negative effects on the
mechanical and durability performance of concrete, the use of PW
as aggregate replacement still elicits interest because it consider-
ably reduces the amount of PW that should be disposed of and the
amount of natural aggregates that should be consumed; therefore,
this practice aligns with environmental sustainability principles.
The current study investigates the potential of using graphene nano-
materials, particularly graphene nanoplatelets (GNPs), to reduce
the negative effects of PW on the durability of concrete. Graphene
nanomaterials are among the most promising nanofillers that can be
used to enhance the properties of cementitious materials. GNPs
are nanomaterials that belong to the graphene family. That is, they
are graphitic materials that consist of multiple graphene sheet layers
with a thickness between 3 nm and 100 nm and a diameter of
micrometers. Some advantages of using GNPs in cementitious
composites include pore filling capability and nucleation site
effects that improve the mechanical properties and durability of
cementitious composites (Guo et al. 2020).
Some researchers have used GNPs to enhance the properties of
cementitious materials. Wang et al. (2016) demonstrated that add-
ing GNPs at 0.05% by weight of the binder increases the flexural
and compressive strengths of cement composites and helps delay
crack propagation in the cement matrix. Several papers have re-
ported the capability of GNPs to suppress and delay microcrack
development and propagation in cementitious matrices. Wang
and Shuang (2018) determined that the addition of GNPs enriches
the compressive and flexural strengths of concrete by 4.3% and
27.4%, respectively. They concluded that GNPs contribute to the
crystallization of calcium hydroxide and the production of more
silica tetrahedron in calcium silicate hydrate (C─S─H) gel and thus
refine the pore structure, reduce the total porosity of the cementi-
tious matrix, and bridge cracks. Du and Dai Pang (2018) researched
the effects of GNPs on the durability of mortar by conducting mer-
cury intrusion porosimetry (MIP). They found that GNPs refine
pore diameters in the mortar matrix and decrease porosity; in par-
ticular, 1% GNPs reduced the diameter and volume of pores by
30% and 37%, respectively. Zhao et al. (2020) reported durability
improvement in mortar with added GNPs in terms of chloride
migration and binding. They attributed this improvement to the
compactness of the mortar due to the pore-filling capability of
GNPs, providing nucleation sites and bridging the effect in the
paste matrix.
Graphene nanomaterials are not cost-effective, and their usage
in concrete increases production cost (ACS Materials 2021). To
offset this problem, the more environment-unfriendly and costly
constituent materials of concrete, such as cement, can be reduced.
By using supplementary cementitious materials (SCMs) as replace-
ments, we can reduce the overall cost of concrete production.
SCMs, such as fly ash, have been used in concrete to reduce cost
and improve environmental sustainability by decreasing the carbon
footprint of cement production. However, the major setback of
using fly ash in concrete is its slower reactivity and lower strength
development during early ages (Siddique 2007; Adamu et al. 2018;
Environment et al. 2018; Herath et al. 2020). Previous studies that
used high-volume fly ash (HVFA) as SCM in concrete reported
decreases in compressive strength, flexural strength, splitting ten-
sile strength, and MoE, which were worse at early ages up to
28 days (Siddique 2004; Duran-Herrera et al. 2011; Huang et al.
2013). However, some studies reported higher strengths in HVFA
concrete compared with normal concrete with further aging (Wang
and Park 2015; de Matos et al. 2019). In addition, fly ash decreases
the durability of concrete by increasing the porosity and volume of
permeable voids, consequently increasing water absorption and
sorptivity (Karahan 2017; Adamu et al. 2020; Herath et al. 2020).
Concrete durability in terms of abrasion resistance also decreases
with the substitution of cement that uses fly ash in concrete as
reported in several studies; this finding is attributed to a reduc-
tion in strength development due to the slow hydration of fly
ash, given that abrasion resistance depends on concrete strength
(Homwuttiwong et al. 2012; Rao et al. 2016; Choudhary et al.
2021).
To lessen the undesirable effects on strength development when
using HVFA in concrete, researchers have used other highly re-
active materials in combination with fly ash to accelerate the
hydration reaction, particularly during initial ages. Nanomaterials,
specifically nanosilica, have been used as an additive to binder con-
stituents in HVFA concrete; studies have found that 3% nanosilica
in HVFA concrete can increase mechanical strength, abrasion re-
sistance, and MoE (Adamu et al. 2018; Gunasekara et al. 2020).

Response-surface methodology (RSM) is one of the most used
statistical methods for developing mathematical models for predict-
ing the properties of cementitious materials on the basis of one or
more input variables. In addition, RSM can be used to optimize the
mix proportions of cementitious materials by setting some target
criteria (optimization goals) based on the variables or properties
of concrete. Another advantage of using RSM in concrete materi-
als is that it minimizes the number of experiments required to
achieve the set objectives, reducing the cost and time of the project
(Montgomery 2017; Adamu et al. 2021a; Chong et al. 2021). RSM
has been applied to mix proportioning, detailed analyses, model
development, and optimization of concrete properties. Chong et al.
(2021) developed models for predicting the strength of eggshell
concrete. They used percentages of eggshell and GGBS, cement
content, fine aggregates, coarse aggregates, water, and superplasti-
cizer (SP) as variables. Their findings indicated that the model de-
veloped to predict the strength of eggshell concrete exhibits great
performance and accuracy. RSM is better in the statistical analysis
of concrete compared with mixed regression. Amiri et al. (2021)
developed mathematical models and multiobjective optimization
for predicting the strength and water absorption of concrete by us-
ing cement content, water-to-cement (w/c) ratio, gravel volume,
and coal waste as independent variables. The developed models
exhibited a high degree of determination and accuracy. Haque et al.
(2021) applied RSM to predict and optimize the properties of
concrete with rice husk ash (RHA) and glass fiber (GF) as SCMs.
The percentages of RHA and GF were used as variables, while
the considered responses were slump, fresh and dry densities, and
compressive and splitting tensile strengths. Their developed models
exhibited good correlation with the experimental data with an
extremely high degree of determination. Zhang et al. (2020) de-
veloped models for predicting the properties and mix design of
pervious concrete with recycled aggregates by using RSM. The
variables were superplasticizer, viscosity-modifying admixture,
and set retarder. The responses were ideal paste thickness, actual
coating thickness, and void content. Their findings indicated that
RSM is a good tool for designing the mix proportion of recycled
aggregate pervious concrete. Poorarbabi et al. (2020) predicted the
compressive strength of concrete on the basis of a nondestructive
test (rebound hammer and UPV) by using RSM. They reported bet-
ter accuracy for the compressive strength models developed using
RSM compared with other models available in the literature.
However, extremely limited literature currently exists on using
PW to improve the ductile and energy absorption capacity of
HVFA concrete or using GNPs to reduce the adverse effects of PW
on the durability of concrete. In addition, no model has yet been
developed using RSM for predicting the properties of HVFA con-
crete by using PW, fly ash, and GNPs as variables. Therefore, the
current study aimed to investigate the effects of GNPs on the
durability and deformation properties of concrete with PW as a
partial substitute for coarse aggregates and HVFA as a partial sub-
stitute for cement. RSM was used to design experiment mixes,
conduct result analysis, and develop models for predicting the unit
weight, MoE, and abrasion resistance of concrete with PW, HVFA,
and GNP contents as variables. Finally, mixture multiobjective
optimization was performed using RSM to determine the optimum
PW, HVFA, and GNP contents that can maximize the durability of
concrete.

Materials
The concrete materials used in the current study included Type I
ordinary Portland cement (OPC), which was in compliance with
ASTM C150M-15 (ASTM 2015), and high-calcium class C fly

GNPs that exhibited the properties listed in Table 2 were used in

spectively. From the energy-dispersive X-ray spectrum shown in

The vertical axis in Fig. 1(c) denotes the amount of energy emitted

Mix Proportions Designed using RSM
A control concrete mix without PW, fly ash, and GNPs was de-
signed for a targeted 28-day compressive strength of 30 MPa
(C30/37) in accordance with the guidelines given in ACI
211.1-91 (ACI Committee 2002) by using the weighing method.
The w/c ratio was 0.38, and NMSA was 19 mm. The mix pro-
portioning of the control mix is provided in Table 3. For the re-
maining mixes, fly ash was used at 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, and
80% substitution by volume of cement. PW was used as a partial
substitute for coarse aggregates at 0%, 15%, 30%, 45%, and 60%.
GNPs were added at 0%, 0.075%, 0.15%, 0.225%, and 0.3% by
weight of binder materials. The mix proportioning was obtained
using RSM.
RSM is a powerful statistical tool that can develop mathematical
relationships between input variables and output responses. A ma-
jor advantage of using RSM for modeling is that it can minimize
the number of experiments required to achieve set results when the
effects of multiple variables on one or more responses must be ex-
amined (Montgomery 2017). Furthermore, RSM is highly suitable
for response optimization by allowing the setting of target criteria
for each variable and response. RSM is also good at interpreting
experimental results represented by nonlinear surfaces. Although
RSM can form general linear models, experimental data typi-
cally contain function curvatures, making linear relations unsuit-
able for describing experimental results. Consequently, polynomial
functions of higher degrees are more appropriate for describing ex-
perimental data and results. The most suitable generalized second-
order polynomial relationship is presented in Eq. (1) (Montgomery
2017; Adamu et al. 2018)

Several model types are available in the RSM design bundle
for determining mathematical relationships between variables and
responses. Among these, the two most widely used are central
composite design (CCD) and Box–Behnken design (BBD). Other
model types include historical data, one factor, optimal, and user-
defined. Model type selection depends on the number of indepen-
dent variables and their variation levels (i.e., number of levels for
each variable). For example, CCD requires a minimum of two var-
iables, and each variable must be varied into five levels at equal
intervals. BBD requires a minimum of three variables, and each
variable must be varied into three levels at equal intervals. One
factor, as the name implies, is used when only one variable exists.
However, the variable can be varied into 3, 5, or 7 levels and rep-
resented by linear, quadratic, or cubic models, respectively. The
historical model type allows preexisting data to be used for mod-
eling, with the number of variables and their variation levels de-
pendent on preexisting data. Among all these model types, CCD is
one of the most suitable because it can predict responses with a
smaller number of experimental results and capture all variables
within specified ranges (Montgomery 2017; Mohammed and
Adamu 2018; Awolusi et al. 2019).
In the current study, Design-Expert software (version 10) was
utilized for RSM experimental designs, mixture proportioning,
model development, and multiobjective optimization. The indepen-
dent variables used were fly ash as a partial substitute by volume of
cement, PW as a partial replacement by volume of coarse aggre-
gates, and GNPs as additive by weight of cementitious materials.
The RSM software generated 19 mixture combinations with these
independent variables, as indicated in Table 3. These mixes were all
prepared and tested in a laboratory. Thereafter, analysis of variance
(ANOVA) was used to determine the interactions between the
independent variables and measured responses.

Specimen Preparation
The quantities of the constituent materials for each mix as listed in
Table 3 were weighed. After weighing, SP was mixed with a part of
the mixing water in a beaker. The measured GNPs were first mixed
into the SP/water suspension and then manually stirred into the
suspension by using a stirring rod until fully dispersed. The beaker
that held this suspension was then placed in an ultrasonicator
(Elmasonic Easy 30, Elma Schmidbauer GmbH, Munich, Germany)
with a capacity of 60 Hz/280 Wand sonicated for 1 h until a homog-
enous mix was achieved. This process was performed to prevent ag-
glomeration and ensure the full dispersion of GNPs, increasing their
effectivity in concrete.
The guidelines outlined in ASTM C192M-15 (ASTM Inter-
national 2015) were followed for batching, mixing, placing, and
curing concrete specimens. First, the dry constituents were mixed
in a rotating pan mixer for approximately 1 min. Then, the GNP/SP
suspension and the remaining mixing water were poured in. Mixing
was continued until a homogenous concrete mix was achieved.
Subsequently, fresh concrete was cast into the required molds in
three layers, with each layer vibrated for approximately 30 s before
another layer was poured. After casting, the specimens were air-
dried for 24 h under controlled relative humidity and 23°C  2°C
before demolding. Finally, the demolded specimens were cured in
water for the required amount of time before testing.

Results and Discussion
Unit Weight
The result for unit weight is presented in Fig. 4. Each mix was la-
beled on the basis of the quantities of variables used. For example,
M0(0P0H0G) is the control mix with 0% PW, 0% HVFA, and 0%
GNPs, while M6(15P20H0.22G) is mix M6 with 15% PW, 20%
HVFA, and 0.225% GNPs. The unit weight of concrete was re-
duced with an increment in the partial substitute for cement that
used fly ash. This finding can be regarded as mixes with higher fly
ash contents having lower unit weight compared with mixes with
lower or no fly ash content. By comparing M1 (30% PW, 40%
HVFA, and 0.15% GNPs) with M18 (30% PW, 80% HVFA, and
0.15% GNPs), M1 has a significantly higher unit weight than M18.
The reduction in weight due to fly ash incorporation is attributed
to the lower specific gravity of fly ash compared with the cement
it replaced because replacement is realized via a volume method
(Barger et al. 2003). In addition, the replacement of cement with
fly ash slows down the hydration reaction because fly ash has a
slower pozzolanic reactivity. This condition reduces the generation
of hydration products, such as C─S─H and calcium-aluminate-
hydrate (C─A─H), which fill the nanopores and densify the concrete
microstructure, leading to a reduction in bulk density/unit weight.
PW exerts a negative effect on the unit weight of HVFA
concrete. For example, when M6 (15% PW, 20% HVFA, and
0.225% GNPs) was compared with M9 (45% PW, 20% HVFA,
and 0.225% GNPs), the latter exhibits lower unit weight values
than the former. Such decrease is attributed to the lower bulk spe-
cific gravity of the PW aggregates compared with natural aggre-
gates they replace (Belmokaddem et al. 2020). Another reason may
be the additional water trapped around PW in the fresh mix. This
water dries up when concrete hardens, leaving voids in the concrete
microstructure and leading to lower unit weight.
By contrast, the addition of GNPs slightly increases the unit
weight of the concrete. For example, the unit weight values of
M9 (45% PW, 20% HVFA, and 0.225% GNPs) are higher than
those of M11 (45% PW, 20% HVFA, and 0.075% GNPs). Such
increase is attributed to the use of GNPs as additive by weight of
cementitious materials, increasing the bulk density of concrete.
Another reason may be the filler capability of GNPs, filling the
pores created by PW, densifying the microstructure of concrete,
and consequently, increasing unit weight.
The unit weight of concrete is important because it exerts an
effect on the overall weight of a structure, which, in turn, has
an effect on foundation size and cost. When used in constructing
a structure, concrete with a lower unit weight and higher compres-
sive strength will be more economical in terms of foundation cost.
In addition, concrete with lower weight and higher strength is more
resistant to seismic activities than heavyweight structures in areas
prone to earthquakes. Therefore, the developed HVFA concrete
with PW and GNPs can be used in applications wherein concrete
with lower weight but higher strength is necessary.

Compressive Toughness
One advantage of incorporating PW into concrete is the improve-
ment of concrete’s energy absorption capacity through an increase
of its ductility and the prolonging of crack initiation time (Saxena
et al. 2018). The load–displacement curves under compressive
loads for concrete mixes are presented in Fig. 7. These curves show
the measurements of the compressive toughness of the concrete
mixes. Compressive toughness can be used to measure concrete
deformability under a compressive load. It is also a measure of
the energy absorption capacity and damage resistance of concrete
under compressive stress (Mo et al. 2014).
The results of the compressive toughness test are presented in
Fig. 8. The partial substitution of natural aggregates with PW im-
proves the compressive toughness of concrete. That is, PW reduces
the brittleness of concrete and thus prevents the catastrophic failure
of concrete if the ultimate load is achieved. The area under the
load–displacement curves for most of the mixes is larger than that
of the control, indicating that the compressive toughness or energy
absorption capacity of these mixes is higher than that of the control
mix. Mixes with high PW content, such as M1, M7, M8, and M18
(30% PW), M5 (60% PW), and M11 and M15 (45% PW) have
higher compressive toughness than the control mix. Such increase
in compressive toughness is attributed to the lower stiffness and
higher elastic nature of PW. Moreover, the fibrous nature of PW
is due to its elongated shape compared with that of the coarse ag-
gregates it replaces. These attributes help slow down crack propa-
gation and increase the ductility of concrete, enhancing its energy
absorption capacity (Saxena et al. 2018; Bahij et al. 2020).
Similarly, mixes with high fly ash contents, such as M12, M14,
and M18, have higher compressive toughness than the control and
mixes with low fly-ash contents, such as M9 and M10. The increase
in toughness caused by fly ash can be attributed to lower strength
development with fly ash that increases porosity and therefore de-
creases stiffness and enhances ductility (Adamu 2018).
Finally, the addition of GNPs reduces the compressive tough-
ness of some concrete mixes. Mixes with high GNP contents, such
as M1, M2, M8, M9, and M10, have lower toughness values than
mixes with PW and low GNP contents, such as M3 and M11. Such
decrease in toughness is due to GNPs densifying and refining the
concrete microstructure, and consequently, increasing the stiffness
and elastic modulus of concrete, but reducing its ductility.
As shown in Figs. 7 and 8, most of the developed HVFA con-
crete mixes with PW and GNPs exhibit higher compressive tough-
ness (energy absorption capacity) than the control (conventional
concrete). Therefore, such mixes can be used in areas where de-
layed crack propagation, higher bending deformation, higher de-
flection resistance, and higher fatigue are required. These areas
include long-span beams subjected to high loads, such as those in
high-rise structures and large-area halls with no columns that can
obstruct the view, in large-area slabs where deflection is a critical
factor, and in pavement applications where the surface is subjected
to flexural and fatigue loads from moving vehicles. In these areas
of applications, concrete can be produced using higher amounts of
PW and fly ash with a lower amount of GNPs.
Abrasion Resistance
The Cantabro abrasion loss results are presented in Fig. 9. Higher
values of Cantabro abrasion loss indicate lower abrasion resistance
of concrete. Abrasion loss increases with the number of revolutions
due to the prolonged weakening of the cement matrix during
abrading.
The 3D plot of abrasion loss after 300 revolutions is shown
in Fig. 10. Abrasion loss increases with fly ash and PW contents.
PW exerts a more significant effect on abrasion loss than fly ash,
as indicated by the steeper slope of abrasion over the PW content
versus that over the fly ash content. The increase in abrasion loss
over the PW content is caused by the poor/weak bonding between
plastic aggregates and the binder matrix, resulting in the easier
wearing out of aggregate particles when they are exposed to abrad-
ing, rubbing, or grinding. In addition, the negative effect of PW
on concrete strength is attributed to the increase in abrasion loss
(Adamu et al. 2018).
As shown in Fig. 9, mixes with lower or no GNPs experienced
higher Cantabro loss values, particularly in conjunction with higher
fly ash and PW contents. The Cantabro loss value for M19 (30%
PW, 40% fly ash, no GNPs) was higher than that of the control mix
by approximately 34%, 44%, and 38% at 100, 200, and 300 rev-
olutions, respectively. Similarly, the Cantabro loss value for M18
(30% PW, 80% fly ash, 0.15% GNPs) was higher than the control
by 32%, 49%, and 47% at 100, 200, and 300 revolutions, respec-
tively. Such abrasion loss increase is due to the influence of fly ash
in slowing down pozzolanic hydration reactivity at earlier ages,
consequently slowing down C─S─H gel production, ultimately
producing weak cement pastes, and leading to easier abrading.

crete strength (Rao et al. 2016; Mohammed and Adamu 2018).
Some of the mixes, as shown in Fig. 9, have lower Cantabro abra-
sion loss than conventional concrete, implying higher abrasion
resistance. Such mixes can be used in application areas where
higher abrasion resistance concrete is required, such as rigid con-
crete and roller-compacted concrete pavements, walkways, drain-
age channels, and canals.

difficult to detect through XRD (Kaur and Kothiyal 2019).

content to an even larger extent. Mixes with GNPs exhibited greater
reduction in CaðOHÞ2 content than mixes with only OPC and fly

GNPs further increased the nucleation site effect and accelerated
the consumption of CaðOHÞ2 and SiO2 in the concrete mixes, as
demonstrated by M6 [Fig. 12(d)], which has a GNP content of
0.225% and significantly lower SiO2 and CaðOHÞ2 signal inten-

accelerated hydration reaction in the matrix and hastened the poz-

thus produce more C─S─H, which is responsible for strength
development. This phenomenon also explains why mixes with
higher GNPs, such as M6, have higher MoE and abrasion resis-

and Shuang (2018). In a related study, Du et al. (2020) indicated

also improves the pozzolanic reaction of fly ash to increase con-
crete strength. Mixes with a high GNP content also had higher

content. For example, the intensity of CaCO3 signals in Figs. 12(e

Fig. 12(c) (low GNP content). This finding is attributed to GNPs’
high carbon content (>95%), as indicated in Table 2, which reacted
with oxygen to form CO2, and further reacted with CaðOHÞ2 from
the cement reactions to form CaCO3.

Multiobjective Optimization
Mixture optimization was performed in Design-Expert (version
10) by using a multiobjective method to determine an optimum
mix combination that minimized Cantabro abrasion loss at 300
revolutions. Criteria were set such that all possible combinations
of variables and responses were captured. The variables were set
within their replacement ranges: PW ranged from 0% to 60%
replacement by volume of coarse aggregates, HVFA ranged from
0% to 80% by volume replacement of cement, and GNPs ranged
from 0% to 0.3% as additive by weight of cementitious materials.
We also set MoE between 19.06 GPa and 43.02 GPa, and unit
weight between 1,968 kg=m3 and 2,315 kg=m3, while Cantabro
abrasion loss at 300 revolutions was minimized. Notably, the re-
sponse intervals were also set to correspond to the experimental
results.
The seven best optimization solutions obtained from the soft-
ware were selected on the basis of their desirability and presented
in Table 8. On the basis of these solutions, the best optimized
HVFA concrete mix contained 12.44% PW as coarse aggregate
replacement, 24.57% HVFA, and 0.279% GNPs by weight of
binder material. This mix can attain a unit weight of 2,314 kg=m3,
an MoE of 43.22 GPa, and a Cantabro abrasion loss of 5.59%
at 300 revolutions with a desirability (degree of adequacy)
of 100%.

Practical Usability
RSM plots are used as practical tools for designing experimental
(real-life) mixes, particularly when many variables are involved
in the material production process. This process will reduce the
number of experimental runs required to achieve the desired prop-
erty (Montgomery 2017). The model equations developed using
RSM in the current study can be used to determine the amount
of variables, such as PW, fly ash, and GNP contents required to
achieve the desired engineering property, i.e., unit weight, MoE,
and abrasion resistance, without conducting new trial experiments,
saving time and cost. For example, when higher MoE is required,
then the model can provide the required combinations of variables,
i.e., the PW, GNPs, and fly ash required to achieve such a target.

Conclusions
In this study, the influences of PW and GNPs on the durability and
deformation properties of concrete were investigated. RSM was
used to design a series of cement mixes, develop models, and con-
duct multiobjective optimization of the ideal cement mix. On the
basis of the experimental data and result analysis, the following
conclusions were drawn:
1. PW, as a substitute for coarse aggregates, and fly ash, as a
replacement for cement, in HVFA concrete negatively affected
unit weight, MoE, and abrasion resistance. Meanwhile, GNPs,
as an additive to HVFA concrete, improved the unit weight,
MoE, and abrasion resistance of concrete.
2. Compressive toughness (energy absorption) improved signifi-
cantly with the replacement of coarse aggregates by PW and
cement by fly ash. It declined with the addition of GNPs.
3. The addition of GNPs to HVFA concrete accelerated the hydra-
tion reaction, igniting the pozzolanic reaction of fly ash through
the nucleation site effect and resulting in densified concrete mi-
crostructures, reduced pore volumes, and improved durability.
4. PW and fly ash negatively affected the durability of HVFA con-
crete through increased porosity and pore volume. By contrast,
GNPs densified concrete microstructure and decreased the po-
rosity and pore volume of concrete.
5. The mathematical models developed using RSM exhibited mod-
erate to high levels of correlation. Thus, they can be used as
good predictors of the considered concrete properties.
6. Multiobjective optimization indicated that a concrete mix with
maximum abrasion resistance was achieved by partially substi-
tuting 12.44% of coarse aggregates with PW, 24.57% of cement
with fly ash, and adding 0.279% GNPs by weight. This mix was
experimentally validated with an error of less than 7%.
